Introduction: The vantage, the framework, the evidence

Preliminary version — for trial in 2026/27 academic year. Content, figures and citations may still change.

A survey of the world economy, and a hypothesis about it.

The view from the centre

In the spring of 2026 the India Meteorological Department published its outlook for the southwest monsoon. NOAA had put the chance of an El Nino developing over May to July at 82 per cent; IMD put it higher and gave a 60 per cent chance that the season rainfall would come in deficient, below 90 per cent of the long-period average. That average was 87 centimetres, across four months that deliver over two thirds of all India’s rain. By early September, newspapers were reporting that there was a monsoon crisis, because of El Nino, and that it was worsening. Media coverage drew a straight line from the original phenomenon, El Nino, through the weak monsoon and a poor harvest to the prospect of hunger and potentially famine. The relationship between El Nino and a weak monsoon is real and it is physical. It is also loose: a correlation of about minus 0.5 to minus 0.6, weaker since the 1980s. In 1997, the Pacific ran one of the strongest El Ninos ever recorded and the monsoon came in normal. A relationship can be genuinely causal, well understood in its mechanism, and still fail to predict any particular year.1

The later steps — not just that a warm Pacific makes a dry season more likely but that a dry season makes a smaller harvest more likely and that in turn a smaller harvest makes hunger more likely — go from physical systems to human ones. Historically, and in the present day, the causal chain runs through granaries, roads, prices, armies and the rules about who has claims on the food produced and thus who may eat. A very long time series from Egypt highlights the importance of understanding how human and physical systems interact. From 622 CE, the height of the Nile flood was measured every year at Roda, because the flood set the land tax: a high flood meant a wide inundation, a large sown area and a full treasury. In measuring a tax base, thirteen centuries of annual hydrology is the by-product. The two lowest floods in the whole series, in 967 and 1200, both coincide with famines. This highlights the importance of physical systems. But the worst subsistence crisis in medieval Egyptian history was 1065–1072, during the Fatimid period, the shidda al-uzma or the great calamity. Across those years, the gauge reads only about half a standard deviation below normal. However, what followed was famine, civil war, the collapse of the military, and the breakdown of the system that moved grain from where it grew to where it was eaten.2

Two systems, and the cost of forgetting either

That is the tension the whole book works inside. Most economics teaching presents the economy as a purely human system, with the physical world kept off the page for simplicity. Across almost all of the five and a half millennia covered here, that will not suffice. What a society could produce was set by how much land it could reach, how reliably the rain came, what the soil would bear and how many people the disease environment left standing. For the Bronze Age those four things are most of the economy itself. And it is important not to push too far that way, because the opposite error can do just as much damage. Geography and climate do not settle outcomes on their own, and a history that lets them do so has taken the people out of it: Egypt in 1065 had the same river it had in 1064. But physical shocks are still economic events and belong in the account: a volcanic eruption that lowers a Nile flood is a fiscal event, and a pandemic that removes a third of a labour force changes the supply of labour for a century. Both physical and human systems are running at every point in this book and it is important to not let one appear to do all the work.

By and large, geography can explain levels but, to explain timing, we need to have something that varies over time. As Chapter 1 shows, with temperatures largely constant and fluctuating only within a narrow band, aspects of the physical environment such as coastlines have been largely constant for the entire span covered, even if important exceptions exist. Mountain ranges, prevailing winds or natural harbours can, in other words, say why a place was persistently rich, or persistently isolated, or persistently expensive to reach. If these do not change from one century to the next, however, they cannot explain in full why something happened in one century rather than the next: a constant cannot explain a variable. Climate does vary, which is what makes it admissible where geography is not, and which is also why it has to be dated precisely enough. That is a demanding level and, at the current scientific frontier, it is one that is rarely passed. This highlights another tension, in this instance between the healthy scepticism a well-trained social scientist should have about causal claims – correlation is not causation – and the inherent limits of what the available evidence can offer.

Standing on the Indian Ocean

Most long-run histories of the world economy are written from an Atlantic perspective, looking outward with the sometimes-accidental implication that it is Europe (and later European settlers in North America) that was the active party and the rest of the world a stage on which European energy plays out. Perhaps the simplest version of world economic history is just that: the world is poor and largely static until, some time between 1500 CE and 1750 CE, Europe stirs and modern prosperity radiates from a single source. In a history of the world economy over the last one or three centuries, this is the full story. For a history of the last millennium it is perhaps closer to the denouement.

Consider the world economy, however, over its full span. Imagine it not from London or New York but from a beach on the Malabar coast or the deck of a dhow running before the monsoon from Aden to Gujarat. For most of recorded history the largest and richest economies, the most commercially developed, were in Asia: Han and Song China, the Abbasid caliphate, Mughal India. The world’s most coveted stores of value, silver and gold, typically flowed toward them rather than away. Europe spent long centuries as a periphery, exporting slaves, furs and raw metal, importing manufactures and ideas, and paying for what it wanted in bullion it could barely produce itself. The period in which Europe genuinely ran the world economy is, in truth, short: concentrated in the two centuries after 1750, and even at its height it rested on the Asian periphery it governed.

The spine: the claim we are testing

The goal of this book is to interrogate the following argument:

Viewed from the Indian Ocean, the world economy spent most of its history centred on Asia — visible in the direction bullion flowed and in where the world’s goods were made. Integration advanced and retreated in waves shaped by climate, disease, technology and money, along durable maritime channels and periodically resurgent overland ones. Europe’s dominance was real and transformative but bounded — concentrated in two centuries after 1750, built partly on the Asian centrality it overturned, and resting at its height on the Indian Ocean periphery it governed. Its timing and depth remain debated, and the East’s aggregate return — measurable but partial, achieved within a Western-built order — can be seen as the long arc closing.

This spine treats European dominance as an interruption or anomaly, rather than a destination or punchline. It is one bounded by four qualifications:

  1. The interruption was consequential, stretching across two transformative centuries;
  2. Its timing and depth are genuinely open, and the debate is reviewed in Chapter 7;
  3. The return east has been partial, being aggregate weight rather than a restoration of per-capita parity; and
  4. It is system-dependent, since the Asian re-emergence happened inside a US-anchored order.

The framework

What makes a place productive

The book relies throughout on one core economic framework as an organising heuristic: the production function. It is used as a scaffold, rather than a model to be solved or estimated. It serves a role each topic, bringing us back to which of the four inputs were doing the work of integration (or disintegration). At its core, what a society can produce depends on four things: its useful knowledge, its physical capital, its people, and its land:

\[Y = f(A, K, L, N)\]

A is technology and useful knowledge, of which machines are the smallest part. A is also technique, crop and animal, navigation, and the ability to write a contract that a stranger will honour. Most of what raised output before the nineteenth century arrived in that form: a wheat variety that ripened a fortnight earlier, a heavier plough and the draught animal that could pull it, a lateen sail that let a hull work into the wind. Seeds and animals are technologies in the strict sense, because they let a society get more out of the same land, and land is the term that was fixed. Institutions live here too, as does trade: a country short of land can import land-intensive goods; a country short of silver can monetise somebody else’s. Like any other technology, voluntary exchange raises what a given endowment will yield.

K is physical capital: ships, warehouses, irrigation works, tools, pack animals, stock in hand. Capital is non-consumed output: something produced and set aside to raise what could be produced later. A merchant ship built with a year of carpenters’ work is an embodiment of choosing to defer consumption today for future consumption. We distinguish here between (real) capital assets, credit and money. Credit is a relationship, in which one party holds a claim, another carries the liability, and purchasing power has moved across time; we know that Mesopotamia had interest-bearing debt by about 3000 BCE, two and a half millennia before the first known coins. Money, on the other hand, is a function. It tells us about what an object (or a claim) does: quote a price, settle a debt, hold value between now and later. Silver by weight does it, and so do cowries, coins, banknotes and a bank deposit. Capital is distinct from both credit and money: it is a thing somebody built and can now use. Whether a ship was paid for with borrowed or saved silver is irrelevant to its impact. The categories overlap — cattle are capital and a store of wealth — but it is worth distinguishing between real and monetary aspects. Given the central role of precious metals, it is helpful to consider how they relate to all three concepts. Silver in the ore body is N, while once refined and cast into an ingot, it is output, Y, and wealth. Promised across time it sits inside a credit relationship. Used to quote a price or settle an obligation it is performing a monetary function. Refined silver was always an economic good; in a great many societies it was also, at the same time, doing monetary work. Coinage standardised a monetary object and certified its content, but it did not invent money. Silver remains perhaps the best single thing to follow across five and a half thousand years, as something that sheds light on the features of the world economy.

L is human capital, labour and people. As described below, population growth (growth in L) is a core measure of success, displaced only in the last topics. But it is also central to understanding changes in integration: voluntary migration, slavery and indenture are ways in which the ratio of L to other endowments changes. It also means human capital: the skills, training and literacy carried in the people themselves. Moving beyond simple headcounts, two workforces of the same size are not the same if one can navigate by the stars, keep a double-entry account, cast bronze to a standard or read a contract. Skill is the part of L that behaves most like A.

N is natural capital: land, energy and resources — the stock of productive assets a society did not build and cannot make more of. In many economics courses, N is folded into K, usually motivated by model tractability (or potentially because land is a small share of costs in some modern settings). Over the full sweep of human history, however, land is fundamental and central. Energy came from land, as food for people and animals and as wood for heat; materials came from land; and the binding constraint on almost every pre-modern economy was how much land it could reach. Putting N as equal to A, K and L is how climate, energy and disease come back into the account as inputs rather than as background. Foregrounding N allows us to highlight also what happens when a resource is concentrated in such a way that its holder can earn a surplus. Those surpluses then become subjects of conflict: geology often sits underneath sources of power. In the modern period, this is visible in oilfields and the coalfields before them. But it can be seen across every topic: from Banda nutmeg and Potosi silver to Baltic timber and amber, and in geographic bottlenecks such as the Straits of Malacca and Suez. Much economic history can be viewed as a fight over N, with the armed trading companies of Chapter 6 the clearest case: they are organisations built to hold chokepoints and secure monopoly access to sources of economic rents.3

At the end of every chapter, there is a review of the elements of the production function: which of A, K, L and N is leading integration or disintegration, and which of them is a binding constraint? We can highlight here two distinct phases. With N roughly fixed and A improving slowly for most of human history, any gain in output was absorbed by more people rather than by richer people. This is, in effect, Malthus restated. Almost every flourishing economy in the first six chapters flourishes by getting bigger — by increasing its ratio of L to N — not by getting richer in per-person terms. The second is the escape: as useful knowledge becomes cumulative and fossil-fuelled, A surges. The N constraint relaxes at the same time, partly because coal substitutes for land as a source of energy and partly because colonial acreage substitutes for domestic acreage as a source of food and fibre. This is when A grew too fast for L to keep up and, instead, output per person started to rise well above subsistence levels.4

The scale of that change, and the limits of what we know about it, are both visible in the growth record. Over the eighteen centuries to 1820, world population multiplied by about 4.6 and income per head by perhaps 1.4; over the two centuries that followed, population multiplied by 7.3 and income per head by 14.8. Measured per generation, population growth after 1820 ran roughly thirteen times faster than before it and income growth at least eighty times faster, and the gap between those two multiples is the escape stated arithmetically. The caveat belongs in the same breath, however, because the income figure is far weaker than the population one. There is no century-by-century estimate of world income per head before 1500, and the pre-1820 rate is a single implied number stretched across the whole span. It is also in large part an assumption, since Maddison assigned a subsistence floor wherever nothing was known. Gregory Clark’s review makes the point sharply: had that floor been set at seven hundred dollars rather than four, there would be no measured growth at all between 1 CE and 1820. What can be defended, then, is that income per head grew by at most about forty per cent over eighteen centuries and possibly not at all, which makes the eighty-fold comparison a floor rather than an estimate.5

That Malthusian regularity is strong rather than universal, and the exceptions are where the interest lies. The best-documented is Roman Egypt, where the wage, price and rent record has been rebuilt from the surviving papyri. Measured in hectolitres of wheat a year — a unit that no currency debasement can get into — the real wage rises from about ten and a half around 75 CE to about fourteen by the middle of the second century, and then falls back to about ten after the Antonine plague. That is intensive growth, in a pre-modern economy, sustained across roughly a century. It also shows what it takes to see such a thing at all: fifty-two observations across two hundred and fifty years, arriving on daily, monthly and annual bases, from the one province that combined a paper habit with a dry climate. The conclusion to draw is not that the Malthusian account fails, but that it is a strong regularity rather than a law, and that the evidence needed to test it survives almost nowhere.6

If the production function says what a society could produce, demand decides whether it does and control of N helps determine who keeps the gains. Capacity is the supply side, and it is what the production function describes. Demand is whether that capacity is used at all, which is not automatic: European households had to acquire a taste for sweetened tea before Asian and Atlantic production had a market to grow into, and Chinese demand for silver as a tax medium is why American mines mattered to a country that never traded with the Americas. Power is who captures the output once it exists, which is where the bottleneck-holder, the armed company and the colonial state come in.

What we mean by a world economy

We should say what the object is before spending eleven chapters locating its centre, and this is harder than it sounds, because most books in this field use the term and never define it. The definition this book works to is deliberately modest. A world economy is a set of regions whose production and prices respond to one another across distances that none of them controls. The test is responsiveness at a distance, and the crucial word is respond: what happens in one place has to alter what is produced, or what things cost, somewhere far enough away that no single authority governs both ends. That is a weaker requirement than a world market, which would need something close to a single price, and a different one from an empire, which is a political object. It is also a stronger requirement than contact, since a diplomatic gift or a shipwrecked sailor establishes contact without establishing anything economic.

The object of interest, then, is the connected part of world output: goods produced for exchange, and above all for exchange over distances long enough that neither end could dictate the terms. Before about 1800 that connected fraction has to be read through population, for reasons set out below, and read with an eye on connectivity rather than head-count alone. Responsiveness itself comes in degrees, and the section on connection below sets that range out. The definition is deliberately weak on institutions, because they change out of all recognition across five and a half thousand years, from temple redistribution to chartered companies to container shipping, and a definition anchored to any of them would exclude most of the period. Responsiveness is the one thing that can in principle be measured at every point on the arc, even where the instrument changes completely, and it sets a bar each period has to clear rather than assume. That is why Chapter 1’s verdict on the centre of gravity is not applicable rather than no: in the third millennium BCE the question does not yet have a subject.

Where the centre of gravity sat, and how sure we are

The spine is a claim about where the weight of the world economy sat, and a claim like that is only as good as its definition of weight. Weight here is output: the centre of gravity is the point world economic activity balances around, and the quantity being balanced is production, with territory, armies and head-counts entering only in so far as they move it. But not all output counts equally. What the spine is about is the connected economy, meaning output that trade enabled, or that was produced for trade, and above all for long-distance trade. A subsistence farmer’s grain is output, and in aggregate it dwarfs everything else in every chapter before the nineteenth century, but it never moved and it cannot move a centre of gravity. So the weighting favours the goods that travelled, the workshops that made them and the ports that handled them, which is why a chapter can call Rome the largest single economy of its age and still put the centre of gravity east of it. The two statements measure different things and both are true.

That choice needs defending, because there is a good objection to it. The connected economy was small, a few per cent of the output of the economies doing it in the periods this book opens with, so weighting it and disregarding the subsistence bulk locates the centre wherever the tradeable goods were made, which was largely Asia. On that reading Asia cannot lose. The reply is that the two measures answer different questions. Total output answers where most production was happening, which before 1800 is very nearly where most people lived, and it says nothing about who could set a price. Connected output answers whose goods other people reorganised themselves to obtain, and on whose terms, which has testable consequences: it predicts where metal accumulated, observable in hoards and ice cores, and it predicts who could refuse a deal. Chapter 2 catches the second one happening, when Indian merchants stopped accepting Roman silver at the moment its content fell. Two honesties follow. For much of the arc the two measures agree, so the choice does less work than the objection implies, and where they come apart the book says which one it is using. The measure also treats the ability to set terms as the thing worth measuring, which is a position, and a reader who prefers aggregate production will read the same evidence and reach a different verdict.

Before about 1800, output is read through population, and the circularity has to be said out loud. In a Malthusian world more food meant more people rather than richer people, so a region’s share of world output tracked its share of world population fairly closely, which makes population a defensible proxy. But most historical output estimates for the pre-modern world were themselves built from population figures and assumed income levels, so when the two series agree before 1800, part of that agreement is a fact about the world and part of it is an artefact of how the numbers were made. Bullion, likewise, indicates without defining. The direction metal flowed is this book’s running tracer, because metal moved toward the workshop, but what a persistent flow reveals directly is where the metal was relatively more valuable and how exchange between the two ends was being settled. It is evidence about monetary demand and about trade imbalances before it is evidence about size.

Every verdict is scored, on a four-point scale plus a null. High means several instruments with different biases give one answer. Moderate means the reading is supported, but one instrument is carrying it. Low means it is a reasonable reading and a different one would not be unreasonable. Not applicable means the question has no meaning in this period yet, which is an honest answer rather than a dodge, and is the entry for Chapter 1. Direction and magnitude are scored separately, because they are separate claims and the evidence for them is usually of different quality. Chapter 2 is the standing example: that Roman metal moved east is about as secure as anything in ancient economic history, and how much of it moved is contested by two orders of magnitude. An answer that gives one confidence level for both has run two claims together.

Applied across all ten verdicts, that scale turns up an asymmetry. Of the nine periods where the question has a subject, seven score high on direction and five score low on scale, so we can usually say which way the weight sat and which way it was moving, and rarely say by how much. That is a property of the evidence rather than of this book: the instruments that establish direction, such as the way metal flowed or what a place exported against what it imported, are robust to being wrong about quantities by a factor of two. It is why Appendix C scores the two separately, and it means a reader who wants numbers will be more often disappointed than one who wants an argument. Two things follow for how the verdicts are made. For nine chapters the centre has to be inferred, from city sizes, trade composition, the direction of bullion and shares of world manufacturing; only in Chapter 10 can it also be computed, using Danny Quah’s distance-weighted average location of world output, which weights all output equally where this book weights connected output and so answers a slightly different question. And there is one switch, announced in advance: Chapters 1 to 6 score aggregate weight, and from Chapter 7 the measure that matters becomes income per head. That switch is the Great Divergence, so the early and late verdicts are not marks on the same ruler, and Appendix C keeps them in one table precisely so that the seam is visible.

The bullion tracer, and why it flips

Before modern capital markets, metal moved toward the workshop. People who made things the rest of the world wanted, Indian cotton and Chinese silk and porcelain, were paid in silver and gold, which pooled where production was most valued. So in antiquity Roman silver drained east to India (Chapter 2), and in the early-modern centuries New World silver flowed east to China (Chapter 5). The tracer even works in reverse where it should: the Abbasid core paid silver north to its slave-and-fur periphery (Chapter 3), mirroring Rome. That reversal matters, because a tracer that only ever points one way is a rule rather than an instrument. One complication arrives before the pattern does, because the earliest traceable silver flow in this book runs the wrong way. In the third and second millennia BCE the metal moves west to east into Mesopotamia rather than east toward a workshop, since at that date the workshop in the later sense does not exist and silver is moving toward demand rather than toward production. That is a reason to state what the tracer assumes rather than to abandon it: it works when one region is making things another region wants badly enough to pay in metal for, and before that condition holds, metal moves for other reasons entirely.

After the nineteenth century the tracer flips, and what flips is what the metal is doing. Under the classical gold standard, gold is not merely accepted by weight at the far end of a voyage; it anchors a settlement system that reaches across borders, and claims denominated in it circulate on the credit of institutions rather than on assay. Follow it then and it leads to the financial core, London and afterwards New York, rather than to the workshop. Note who underwrote that core: India’s colonial drain helped finance Britain’s payments deficits (Chapter 8), sourced in the periphery and intermediated by the centre.

Most of the economy was never priced

For almost all of the period this book covers, most of what was produced was never bought or sold. Output sits in three layers. The bottom and by far the largest is subsistence and obligation: what a household grew and ate, plus what it handed over as rent, tribute, tithe or labour dues. That second category has no clean modern counterpart, and it matters, because a peasant handing over a third of the harvest is transferring real output without any price being formed anywhere. Above that sits regional exchange, in local markets, at prices that were real but seldom recorded. Above that, thinnest of all, sits long-distance trade. Our evidence comes overwhelmingly from the top layer, because that is the layer that generated records: customs registers, merchant letters, tax farms, port dues. So when a source gives us a number, the first question is always what the number is a number of.

Confuse the layers and it goes badly. Chapter 2 has to ask how large the Roman eastern trade was, and the published estimates of the annual value of Rome’s Indian Ocean imports run from about 920 million sesterces to about 17,640 million, where the high end is roughly the size of the most-cited estimate of the entire Roman economy. Nobody involved is careless: the estimates differ because each takes the tax assessment of one documented cargo and multiplies it by an assumed number of ships, and the assumptions differ. But notice what has happened. A number drawn entirely from the thin top layer, the customs record, is being compared with a number for all three layers at once, and the comparison only works if we know what fraction of Roman output ever passed through a market at all. It did not, and we do not.

The four questions, and where they meet the framework

The book asks the same four questions of every period. They organise every chapter, and they are the method the book works with.

ImportantThe four motivating questions
  • Q1 — Direction. Is the system integrating or disintegrating? Two refinements the evidence forces on the simple cycle: integration breeds fragility, since the links that carry goods carry shocks; and disintegration has winners, since a fracturing core can loosen its grip on a periphery.
  • Q2 — Channels. Which routes carry the integrating weight? Three run through this book: the maritime Indian Ocean, the desert-and-oasis corridor, and the steppe. The ocean is the durable artery; the overland channels had one true climax, the Mongol Pax of Chapter 4.
  • Q3 — Modes. What crosses the connection? The answer is read off the framework, input by input. The real flows are output itself, useful knowledge, people and capital assets — Y, A, L and K. Alongside them run monetary and financial flows: settlement assets, and claims and liabilities. Bullion can sit on both sides at once, which is why its direction serves as the book’s running tracer of the centre of gravity.
  • Q4 — Europe. What is Europe’s role: peripheral, entrant, dominant, one of several cores? And how does it change? The book asks this in every period and answers it from the evidence.

Q4 is the odd one out, and readers are entitled to ask why Europe gets a question to itself. The answer is not that Europe is more important than the other actors, since for most of this book it is plainly less so. It is that Europe is where the prior sits: we have absorbed a version of the story in which European development is the main sequence and everything else is background. The way to test that prior is to make it a question, with the possible answers laid out in advance and none of them privileged. Asked that way, the answer changes across the ten chapters in a pattern that is genuinely interesting, and in Chapter 1 it changes twice, since Bronze Age Europe turns out to be a resource periphery supplying tin to an Asian-centred system, which is a much stranger finding than either “central” or “absent”.

The four questions and the production function are two axes of a single piece of apparatus. The production function is vertical, asking what makes a place productive; the four questions are horizontal, asking how places connect; and they meet at N. That gives a definition of integration with some economics in it. To integrate is to relax a binding constraint by drawing on somebody else’s inputs: importing land-intensive goods for want of land, monetising foreign silver for want of good mines, importing labour that cannot be raised at home, borrowing technology that was invented elsewhere. Q3’s four flows are the four inputs. Goods are N embodied, in the sense that a cargo of grain or tin is somebody else’s land arriving in usable form; technology is A, people are L, and capital is K. The mapping is close enough to be useful and loose enough to be worth checking in each period rather than assuming.

Connection and integration

The framework asks what makes a single place productive. The rest of the book asks how places reach each other, and that is a separate question, with its own instruments and its own characteristic errors.

From contact to market integration

Connection between two economies comes in degrees, and four will do for the whole book. Contact is the weakest: a diplomatic gift arrives, or a shipwrecked sailor, or a single bead, and nothing about either economy has changed. Diffusion is the arrival of something that stays, such as a crop, an animal, a technique or a design, and a transfer that happens once can outweigh a trade that happens every year, since neither the chicken out of Southeast Asia nor the potato into Europe ever needed a second voyage. Sustained connection means the flow repeats: ships come back, a route acquires a season and a set of prices, and both ends start to plan around it. Market integration is the strongest and the rarest, with production and prices in one place responding to conditions in another closely enough that a shock at one end shows up at the other.

Where along that range the phrase “world economy” begins to apply is a live argument. Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giraldez date globalisation to 1571 and the Manila galleon, on the criterion that every populated continent was then in continuous and consequential exchange. Kevin O’Rourke and Jeffrey Williamson answer that contact without converging prices is not integration, which puts the date after 1820. Two definitions, two and a half centuries apart, argued across three papers in the same journal. This book runs both instruments and says in each period which one it is using, because taking either one strictly destroys the subject: on the price test the first six chapters have nothing to measure, and on the diffusion test every stray bead becomes evidence of a world economy.7

7 Sources: O’Rourke and Williamson, “When did globalisation begin?”, European Review of Economic History 6 (2002), 23-50, doi:10.1017/S1361491602000023; Flynn and Giraldez, “Path dependence, time lags and the birth of globalisation”, EREH 8 (2004), 81-108, doi:10.1017/S1361491604001066; O’Rourke and Williamson, “Once more: when did globalisation begin?”, EREH 8 (2004), 109-17, doi:10.1017/S1361491604001078. Read more: Chapter 5, where the Manila galleon is the case in point.

The two yardsticks

The book measures two different things and keeps them apart, and half the disagreements in this field are two people scoring on different instruments without noticing. Integration is the question set out above, measured on whichever of the two instruments the period allows: diffusion where there are no prices, price convergence once there are, with the switch around Chapter 3. Success is the other yardstick, and it has two settings. Quantity, or extensive growth, means more people, bigger cities, larger total output. Quality, or intensive growth, means sustained income per head, which is the modern escape and, on this timescale, a very recent one. Almost every thriving economy in this book is thriving on the first setting: Han China, Song China, Mughal India and Qing China were all much larger than their contemporaries and none of them richer per person in the way the phrase implies today. An answer that says China was richer than Europe in 1700, without saying whether it means in aggregate or per head, has not yet said anything.

Trade costs, and what price convergence cannot see

The strongest instrument in the book is price convergence, and what it does is narrower than its reputation. A good rarely costs the same in two places. The test is that the gap between the two prices is bounded by the cost of moving the good, and narrows as that cost falls, so a stable price gap between two trading places is the cost of integration, quoted. Trade settles where the gap the market will bear equals the trade cost: freight, plus tolls, plus risk, plus whatever has to be paid for protection. That last term is why integration in this book is as much a political variable as a technological one, and it means a narrowing gap can arise three ways. Demand can rise where the good is bought; supply can rise where it is sold; or the cost of carriage can fall. Only the third is integration, so rising volumes are not evidence of integration. Converging prices are.8

8 Sources: Shiue and Keller, “Markets in China and Europe on the eve of the Industrial Revolution”, American Economic Review 97 (2007), for the method applied comparatively. Read more: Chapter 6 and Chapter 8, where the test does most of its work.

The blind spot follows directly, and it is the one that matters in the early chapters, since before roughly 500 BCE we are working almost entirely inside it. Chapter 1 does contain one place where the test can almost be run, and it shows what the instrument feels like when it works. In the Old Assyrian trade of the early second millennium BCE, tin bought at Assur cost about 6.7 shekels of silver per hundred shekels of tin, and the same tin sold in Anatolia at about 14.3, a gap of roughly 2.1 times across about a thousand kilometres of donkey caravan. It is not arbitrage, because arbitrage is riskless and simultaneous and this was neither: the merchant financed the goods, waited a hundred days, and bore the risk of losing everything to bandits or a change of ruler. The gap is the cost of carriage, quoted, in a world with no coinage and no market in the modern sense, and it did not close. It persisted for generations, which says the costs were structural rather than a temporary friction somebody was about to compete away.

Integration breeds fragility

The first refinement to Q1 earns a section of its own, because it is the closest thing this book has to a general finding. The links that carry goods carry shocks: a network that moves pepper efficiently moves pathogens efficiently, and a system whose parts have specialised in what they do best is a system in which no part can feed itself when the connections fail. The pattern shows up at every scale. The Black Death is the clearest case, moving along the Mongol roads that had just made overland trade viable for the first time in centuries, and the Late Bronze Age collapse, the Antonine plague and the financial crisis of the 1930s all repeat it. The lesson is not that integration is bad, which would be a strange thing to conclude from a book about it. It is that integration and fragility are the same property observed at different moments, and that a period’s prosperity and its vulnerability are usually built out of the same links. When a chapter reports a system at its peak, the next question is what that peak has made it unable to survive.

The evidence, and what it will bear

Across the periods covered in this book, we have to use different types of instruments. But the nature of any given instrument decides what can be claimed: a chapter using ice cores can say things a chapter resting on customs registers cannot, and vice versa. In this section, we take the production function framework and outline what each input, each stock of wealth, has for its own evidence base, as well as the potential traps in each.

Reading N: land, climate and resources

The evidence for N is mostly physical and mostly not written by anybody. Ice cores from Greenland and the Alps record atmospheric deposition year by year, so a layer of lead in the ice is a by-product of somebody smelting silver out of galena a continent away. Speleothems, which is to say stalagmites, record rainfall in their oxygen isotopes. Lake sediments, pollen cores, marine cores off river deltas and tree rings all give climate at various resolutions, and ore bodies have chemical fingerprints, so a metal object can sometimes be traced to the ground it came out of. The characteristic failure is dating, and the second is scale. A stalagmite might be dated to within a century, which sounds precise until the question is whether a drought preceded a civilisation’s collapse or followed it. The cleanest instance in this book is the drying around 2200 BCE, where three independent archives agree on the direction and their error bands are each as wide as the three-century gap they are being asked to bridge, so none of them can order the climate event against the settlement change it supposedly caused. Chapter 1 runs that argument out against the Harappan de-urbanisation, which post-dates the climate event by some three hundred years. The scale problem is related, since a core measures its own catchment, not the world.

Reading L: people

Population is reconstructed from settlement survey, from cemetery demography, from skeletal and stature evidence, and increasingly from ancient DNA. Isotopes in tooth enamel give something different again: strontium and oxygen laid down in childhood record where a person grew up, and enamel never remodels, so the molars carry an ordered record of where somebody was at particular ages. The majority of early population figures in this book are from an area multiplied by an assumed number of people per unit of area; the assumed coefficient does most of the work. Change the coefficient and the answer changes proportionally; change which line on the ground counts as the edge of the settlement and it changes by more than that. Appendix D sets out two worked cases: an urbanisation rate for Sumer where two careful scholars differ by a factor of three, and a population for Pataliputra where the published estimates span a factor of about thirteen without anybody disputing any evidence at all.

Isotopes and DNA look like two ways of measuring the same thing and are not, because isotopes measure residence and DNA measures descent. A merchant who sailed to Carthage, traded for a season and sailed home is visible to the first instrument and completely invisible to the second, because he left no descendants there; his grandchild, born in Carthage to a local mother, is the exact reverse. This is why the finding that western Mediterranean Punic populations carry almost no Levantine ancestry shows that Phoenician settlers did not dominate the gene pool, and does not show that Phoenicians did not go there. Both report lower bounds, and where they disagree the disagreement is itself a measurement: the ratio of circulation to settlement, which is a real economic quantity we would otherwise have no way to see.9

9 Sources: Ringbauer et al., “Punic people were genetically diverse with almost no Levantine ancestors”, Nature 643 (2025), 139-47, doi:10.1038/s41586-025-08913-3. Read more: Chapter 2 and Chapter 3, where both instruments are used.

Reading K: capital, money and credit

One heading covers three things the framework keeps apart, and the reason is the archives rather than the economics. Hoards, stray coin finds, die studies and bullion shipment records are evidence about money; loan tablets and partnership contracts are evidence about credit; ship timbers, irrigation surveys and warehouse accounts are evidence about capital itself. In practice they arrive in the same box, because the people who wrote down one of them wrote down the others on the same tablet, and above all they arrive in merchant archives: the letters, loans and accounts of people doing business. The categories still have to be kept apart, because coin is evidence about settlement and says nothing about how many ships there were. The characteristic failure is that archives survive by accident, and disproportionately by catastrophe. Mesopotamia’s cuneiform tablets were unbaked clay, and what fired them hard enough to last four thousand years was the burning of the palaces that held them, so we read those economies in detail because they were destroyed. A place that was never sacked, or whose records were kept on palm leaf or paper in a wet climate, is simply harder for us to read, and legibility is a poor proxy for commerce. Coin hoards carry their own version of the same problem, since a hoard dates when somebody decided to bury money and did not come back for it, which is a fact about insecurity as much as about circulation.

A second and subtler difficulty is that coins are recycled: the metal in a coin struck in 200 CE may have been mined in 50 BCE, melted down twice and alloyed with something else on the way, so a chemical analysis of coinage reports what was in the melting pot rather than what came out of the ground. Chapter 2 puts an ice core and a coin series side by side for exactly this reason, because the ice records what was smelted and the coins record what was refined and recycled.

Reading A: technology and technique

A is read from the objects themselves, and from the sciences that interrogate them: archaeometallurgy, lead and tin isotope provenancing, residue analysis, and radiocarbon dating of the contexts things are found in. Its characteristic failure runs the opposite way to the others, and it is a cheerful one, because better dating destroys more claims than it creates. When a technique improves, the usual result is that something long believed turns out to rest on a specimen that is intrusive, or younger than its context, or misidentified. The cleanest example is the chicken. Domestic chickens were for decades said to originate in the Indus Valley, and a systematic re-dating programme found that every European Neolithic and Bronze Age specimen tested was intrusive, moving the earliest secure domestic chickens to Thailand and about a thousand years later. Nothing was fabricated and nobody was careless; the instrument got sharper and a claim fell over. That is how to read the confident sentences in older books, including good ones. A claim about origins established before radiocarbon calibration, or before a genome could be sequenced from a tooth, has not been refuted so much as never tested, and the pattern of the last twenty years is that most such claims do not survive.

What each proxy can and cannot support

The limits are worth setting out in one place, because most over-claiming in this field is one of these four moves.

What the standard proxies will and will not bear.
Proxy What it supports What it does not
Diffusion of goods Contact, and the geographic range of a network Volume, frequency, price, or who profited
Direction of carriage Relative shipping cost; which end wanted the other’s goods How the surplus was divided between the two ends
Population Aggregate scale, under a Malthusian assumption Income per head, ever
Settlement counts An upper bound on decline The decline itself, since the record-keeping collapses too

The fourth row is the one most often missed. When a state falls apart, the number of attested settlements drops, but the thing that produced the attestations, an administration writing things down, fell apart at the same time. So a fall in recorded settlements is a composite of real decline and lost visibility, and it gives a ceiling on the first rather than a measurement of it.

Absence of data is not absence of activity

What has survived reflects some combination of conventions about record-keeping and the accidents of climate and preservation, not economic weight. Organic records rot quickly in the humid monsoon tropics; they may survive better in cool dry Europe and arid Egypt, so the surviving record systematically under-documents the ocean world this book is written around. Because the direction of that bias is known, we should treat the figures we have as lower bounds: trade was at least as large as the record shows. But it does not license filling the gap with a conclusion: “the evidence is biased against parts of Asia” is true, but this does not automatically mean that “Asian trade was much larger”.

Distinguishing the map of evidence from the map of production

One commodity demonstrates the problem: black pepper, which is south Indian. Peppercorns that have been recovered from the Roman period survived in one of three ways: desiccation, in the hyper-arid Egyptian desert (at Berenike and Myos Hormos), waterlogged in anaerobic mud in places like Germany, Britain and France, or mineralised in cesspit fill in Campania. But where pepper was actually grown, none of those three conditions holds. So until very recently, essentially none of the finds were from the producing region. In this case, it is easy to read the map as one of trade and distribution, not one of production but the lesson may well hold for other products as well. In short, across this whole book, and especially for earlier periods, the map of the evidence is not the map of the world economy; rather, it is the map of preservation conditions. A set of products with very poor chances of preservation are textiles. Goods made from cotton, silk and wool rot in every climate that grew them.10

10 Sources: Cobb, on the recovery contexts of Roman-period pepper, citing Cappers (2006), Livarda (2011), and Robinson and Rowan (2015). Read more: Chapter 2.

The oldest annual series in the world are tax records

The longest continuous annual measurements we have of anything physical reflect human priorities: The Nilometer measure of the Nile flood runs annually (with some gaps) from 622 CE because flood height set the Egyptian land tax. Similarly, data on the Beaune grape harvest run from 1354 because the town council had to pay the pickers on a date. Data on Kyoto cherry blossoms or Lake Suwa’s freeze similarly have motivations related to human institutions. But because they were measuring a tax base or a wage bill, the geography of the pre-modern climate record is closer to a map of fiscal and institutional capacity. Further, it degrades when the polity degrades. The Nilometer holds between seventy-seven and a hundred readings per century up to 1470, and then thirty-two in the sixteenth century and thirty-one in the seventeenth. The density of a series is itself a datum about the state that produced it. And what the series tells us needs care: the apparent upward drift after about 1300 reflects the silting up of the river bed, rather than a wetter Nile.11

11 Sources: Toussoun (1925), digitised by Koutsoyiannis et al. (2013); Kondrashov, Feliks and Ghil (2005) on the series’ spectral structure. Read more: Chapter 3.

Grading claims we read

Because the evidence is so uneven, it will be necessary to grade individual claims, as well as verdicts. We can consider a claim to be: secure when a named source has been read and says it; probable when it takes one step of inference, or rests on a synthesis rather than the original study; and contested when specialists disagree in print. And it is single-source when one authority stands behind it and nobody has independently confirmed it; that is not a criticism so much as a warning to the reader. Related, agreement between two documents that share an ancestor is not evidence of the strength of a claim, which is why a claim has to be traced back to the source that first measured or observed the thing. In preparing Chapter 2, this book compiled a register of claims about early historic South Asia. On the forty-five entries whose subject is genuinely South Asian, fewer than one in six came out secure and nearly one in three contested. There is value in that ratio: it marks the limits of what can be known about the Mauryan and post-Mauryan economy at the moment.

An example, from Java, shows another value, intrinsic to the scientific method. In 2023, a team published a study of Gunung Padang in West Java arguing that the hill is actually a construction, begun by at least 14,000 BCE, and “potentially the oldest pyramid in the world”. The journal retracted it in March 2024, because the radiocarbon dating “was applied to soil samples that were not associated with any artifacts or features that could be reliably interpreted as anthropogenic”. The laboratory work was, undoubtedly, careful but what failed was the join between the number and the claim. The age belongs to the soil; the construction event was inferred separately and then had the soil’s age attached to it. The authors still disagree with the retraction, so the case is still open.12

12 Sources: Natawidjaja et al., “Geo-archaeological prospecting of Gunung Padang buried prehistoric pyramid in West Java, Indonesia”, Archaeological Prospection 31:2, doi:10.1002/arp.1912, retracted at doi:10.1002/arp.1932 on 18 March 2024. Read more: Ernenwein, “Retraction and reflection”, ArcheoSciences 49-1 (2025), 607-10, the editor’s own account of the case.

The instruments improve, and that is the shape of the book

Set against the chapters, the four evidence bases show a progression. For Chapter 1 there are no price series at all. Integration has to be read from the diffusion of objects and standards and the verdict on the centre of gravity is not applicable. From Chapter 2 there are coins, quoted prices in a handful of documents, and the first physical output series, so direction can be argued and magnitude cannot. From Chapter 3 to Chapter 6 the merchant archives thicken and price series begin to run continuously in a few places, which is enough to test convergence over decades. From Chapter 7 there are wages, prices and the first constructed national accounts, and the argument shifts from where the weight sat to why it moved. By Chapter 9 and Chapter 10 there are trade statistics collected by states for their own purposes, and finally a centre of gravity that can be computed rather than inferred. Two things follow. The first is that the book’s later verdicts are better evidenced than its earlier ones, which is stated in Appendix C chapter by chapter. The second is the one people forget: better evidence does not make the later arguments less contested. Chapter 7 has more data behind it than any chapter before it and contains the book’s least settled question. The quantity of evidence and the level of disagreement are close to independent of one another, and assuming otherwise is how a reader ends up trusting a well-measured claim more than it deserves.

One cargo, and what four questions can do with it

The apparatus is easier to follow once it has been run on a real object, and there is a good one to hand. In the late fourteenth century BCE a ship went down off Uluburun, near Kas on the southern Turkish coast, and it was found in 1982. It is the richest single deposit of Bronze Age trade goods ever recovered, and it is a dataset of one. Aboard were about ten tonnes of copper, as 354 four-cornered “oxhide” ingots and 121 bun-shaped ones, and roughly a tonne of tin, which is close to the ten-to-one ratio bronze needs, so the cargo is about eleven tonnes of alloy in kit form. With it went about 175 glass ingots, a tonne of terebinth resin in at least 149 Canaanite jars, 135 Cypriot vessels, ebony and ivory, Baltic amber and Mesopotamian bitumen: one hull carrying the products of at least nine or ten cultures. The four questions can then be put to it. Q2, channels, it answers well: this is a maritime cargo moving counter-clockwise around the eastern Mediterranean, and the amber alone establishes a link running to the Baltic. Q3, modes, it answers partly, since the flow is overwhelmingly goods, which is N embodied, and the merchants’ weight sets aboard suggest private commercial calculation alongside gift exchange. Q1, direction, it barely touches, because one wreck is a point and direction requires a series. And Q4, Europe, it raises without settling, because where the tin came from is precisely what two laboratories now disagree about.

What it cannot tell us is the more useful half. It cannot say how many such ships sailed, or how often, or what any of it cost, or who profited. It cannot even settle whether this was commerce or diplomacy, which is the question Chapter 1 takes up with the evidence on both sides. And there is a preservation trap sitting inside it. The 135 Cypriot vessels aboard are roughly twice the number known from the whole Aegean for the entire Late Bronze Age. If one wreck can double the record, then the record was never a sample of the trade. It is a sample of what happened not to be lost.13

Using this book

Three ledgers to keep

Ten topics can be ten surveys or one argument, and the difference is whether things are written down along the way. Keep three running lists, one line per chapter. The centre-of-gravity ledger records where the weight is, whether it is moving, and how confident the reading is, on the four-point scale above. The input ledger records which of A, K, L and N is doing the work, and which is binding. The natural-system beat records the climate, energy or disease force in play, and is there to stop the physical world becoming background scenery. Three short rows for each topic, kept as the topics go by, and by Chapter 10 the book’s argument will have been assembled by hand, which is a considerably better position to argue with it from than having read it.

Six threads that run the length of the book

Everything above recurs, and it recurs as six threads. Naming them together is a way of noticing what a straight chronology would hide.

  1. Centre of gravity, with a verdict and a stated confidence in every chapter, collected in Appendix C.
  2. Quantity to quality, the second yardstick, resolving at Chapter 7.
  3. Follow the silver, the early-modern form of the bullion tracer. The same American metal met different monetary conditions and had different fates: Asia absorbed and specialised, the Ottomans inflated, West Africa debased and depleted, and Europe turned a monetary loss into armed-trade and financial power. That last fate is the bridge to the divergence.
  4. Measurement of integration, on instruments that improve from diffusion through bullion flows and prices to a computed centre of gravity.
  5. Forces: climate, disease, technology and policy, two of them natural.
  6. Data survival and bias, and whose eyes we are seeing through.

A note on method

Two commitments follow, and they are why the subtitle is data and debates.

  • Data. The book leans on numbers, because numbers are how a claim like the spine gets adjudicated rather than asserted. Figures and tables are reproducible, generated from datasets that can be downloaded and re-run. But the data thin out sharply further back, and they are biased toward the literate, fiscal, European end of the record. A chapter on the Bronze Age and a chapter on the twentieth century are not equally evidenced, and the book says which is which.
  • Debates. Where the scholarship is genuinely contested, and the standing examples are when the Great Divergence began and why, whether interwar disintegration was man-made or inevitable, and when globalisation really started, the book presents the disagreement rather than resolving it out of sight. Disputed figures are flagged and taught rather than silently settled.

The complaint this book is built on is not its own invention. David Northrup observes that textbooks “almost invariably” break their coverage at 1500, and Merry Wiesner-Hanks, introducing the Cambridge World History, names the problem in her own preface while conceding that four of that series’ nine books are given to 1400 and after. Northrup pushes further back, in a form that reaches this book’s own starting line: the deeper exclusion is the convention of beginning with Sumer and with writing, which removes between ninety-five and ninety-nine per cent of the human past before the story starts. There are honest defences of that imbalance. David Christian argues the demographic case, that perhaps eighty per cent of all human lives were lived between the Palaeolithic and 1750, and the counter is that a historian can work only with evidence, which accumulates toward the present. Michael Lang, in the same volume, goes after this book’s method rather than its coverage, arguing that periodising by chronology smuggles in assumptions about what changes and when.14

14 Sources: Northrup, “Globalization in historical perspective”, in The Cambridge World History, vol. 1 (2015), pp. 110-31, at pp. 111-12 and 123; Wiesner-Hanks, preface to vol. 1, pp. xvii and xix; Christian, “Introduction: the constructions of a global past”, vol. 1, pp. 22-23; Lang, “Evolution, rupture, and periodization”, vol. 1, pp. 84-109, at pp. 96-97. Read more: Sachsenmaier, “The evolution of world histories”, vol. 1, pp. 56-83.

This idea of this book is to test the claim made here. This means that it is possible to read the evidence assembled here and reach a different verdict. The book aims to highlight the places where disagreement is most likely: for example, where an argument rests on a single instrument or the verdicts is uncertain. The derivations in Appendix D show that a headline figure turns out to be a coefficient multiplied by an area and thus the choice of coefficient is critical. A reader who finishes convinced that European dominance was longer, or deeper, or earlier than the spine allows should ask which of those places would have to be attacked to make that case. That is the idea behind setting out the hypothesis: being clear about what it would take to refute it.
