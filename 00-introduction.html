Introduction: The vantage, the framework, the evidence

Preliminary draft — under review. Published for review; content, figures and citations may still change.

A claim to test, not a creed to defend.

Start with a headline you believe

Every spring, Indian newspapers carry a version of the same story. The monsoon will be weak this year, the story says, because of El Nino. Farmers should expect a poor kharif season, food prices will rise, and rural demand will soften. The story is not silly. It is written by competent journalists from official forecasts, and most economists reading it would nod along.

Nod along, and then look at what the sentence actually did. The Pacific was warm. Warm Pacific years are, on average, drier over India. A drier year means a smaller harvest. A smaller harvest means higher prices and weaker demand. Each of those steps is a tendency, and the headline welded four tendencies into a chain and then attached the word “because” to the front of it. Nothing in the chain is a cause of anything in particular. It is a set of probabilities wearing the clothes of an explanation.1

1 Sources: the structure of the seasonal forecast follows the India Meteorological Department’s long-period-average convention, in which a season below about 90 per cent of the long-period average is classed as deficient, and the June-to-September monsoon supplies roughly three-quarters of India’s annual rainfall. Read more: the module’s Topic 0 slides carry the current year’s figures, which change annually and are deliberately not printed here.

Be precise about how loose the chain is, because the looseness is the lesson. The correlation between El Nino and a weak Indian monsoon is real and it is physical, not a statistical accident. It is also only about minus 0.5 to minus 0.6, and it weakened after the 1980s. In 1997 the Pacific ran one of the strongest El Ninos ever recorded and the monsoon was normal. A relationship can be genuinely causal, well understood in its mechanism, and still fail to predict any individual year. When that is true, it is not an explanation of any individual year either.

We will meet this problem in every chapter of this book, usually with worse data and higher stakes. Did the monsoon fail and empty the cities of the Indus? Did a plague end the Roman peace? Did climate cause the Bronze Age collapse? Students write those sentences confidently, and the sentences have the same shape as the headline: a real physical relationship, a real historical event, and a “because” inserted where the evidence supports only a “sometimes, on average”.

So here is a case that runs the other way, and it is the best one in the book. Egypt kept a continuous annual record of the Nile flood from 622 CE, because flood height set the land tax. The two lowest floods in thirteen hundred years, in 967 and 1200, both coincide with famines, which is what you would expect. Then comes the Fatimid famine of 1065 to 1072, the shidda al-uzma, the great calamity, one of the worst subsistence crises in Egyptian history. The gauge says the water was about half a standard deviation below normal. In other words, nearly fine. What produced the famine was civil war, the collapse of the military, and the breakdown of the system that moved grain from where it grew to where it was eaten. The harvest was not the binding problem. The entitlement to the harvest was.2

2 Sources: the Roda Nilometer series, ed. Toussoun (1925), digitised by Koutsoyiannis et al. (2013); on the Fatimid crisis and its non-climatic character, the discussion in this book’s Chapter 3. Read more: Koutsoyiannis (2013), doi:10.1080/02626667.2013.804626.

Your intuition was reasonable. The evidence sometimes refuses it. The rest of this introduction is the apparatus for telling those two situations apart.

The view from the other shore

Imagine the world economy not from London or New York but from a beach on the Malabar coast, or the deck of a dhow running before the monsoon from Aden to Gujarat. It is a deliberately awkward place to stand, because almost every long-run history of the world economy is written from somewhere else: from the North Atlantic, looking outward, with Europe as the active party and the rest of the world as the stage on which European energy plays out. Tell the story that way and its shape is settled before the evidence arrives. The world is poor and static until, around 1500 or 1750, Europe stirs, and modern prosperity radiates from a single source.

Stand on the Indian Ocean shore instead and a different shape appears. For most of recorded history the largest and richest economies, and the most commercially developed, were in Asia: Han and Song China, the Abbasid caliphate, Mughal India. The world’s most coveted store of value, silver and gold, flowed toward them rather than away. Europe spent long centuries as a periphery, exporting slaves, furs and raw metal, importing manufactures and ideas, and paying for what it wanted in bullion it could barely produce itself. The period in which Europe genuinely ran the world economy is short, concentrated in the century or so after 1750, and even at its height it rested on the Asian periphery it governed.

The default reverses things more thoroughly than that summary suggests. In 1000 CE the largest cities in the world were Baghdad, Kaifeng and Cordoba, and Cordoba is the only one most European readers will place. The most sophisticated commercial instruments of the age, the partnership contract, the bill of exchange, deposit banking, were being used across the Islamic world and in Song China before they appeared in Italy. And the goods that Europeans crossed oceans to obtain in the fifteenth century had been moving across the Indian Ocean for a thousand years in ships that Europeans did not build, on routes they did not know, in a trade whose prices they did not set.

This book takes that view seriously enough to build a whole history around it. But the vantage point is a tool. It earns its place by making an anomaly visible, and it should be dropped the moment it stops paying. The argument it generates is a claim, and the book’s job is to test that claim period by period against what the evidence will bear, and to be candid about where the evidence is thin and where serious scholars disagree.

What we mean by a world economy

We should say what the object is before spending eleven chapters locating its centre, and this is harder than it sounds. Most books in this field use the term and never define it, which is convenient, because every definition either excludes most of the period or admits almost everything.

The definition this book works to is deliberately modest. A world economy is a set of regions whose production and prices respond to one another across distances that none of them controls. Not a world market, which would require something close to a single price; not an empire, which is a political object; and not merely contact, since a diplomatic gift or a shipwrecked sailor establishes contact without establishing anything economic. The test is responsiveness at a distance, and the crucial word is respond: what happens in one place has to alter what is produced, or what things cost, somewhere far enough away that no single authority governs both ends.

That definition is weak on institutions and strong on responsiveness, and it is weak on institutions on purpose. The institutions change out of all recognition across five and a half thousand years, from temple redistribution to chartered companies to container shipping, and a definition anchored to any of them would exclude most of the period. Responsiveness is the one thing that can, in principle, be measured at every point on the arc, even if the instrument for measuring it changes completely. It also sets a bar the book has to clear in each period rather than assume, which is why Chapter 1’s verdict on the centre of gravity is not applicable rather than no. In the third millennium BCE the question does not yet have a subject.

The spine: the claim we are testing

Here is the argument in one paragraph. The rest of the book interrogates it.

Viewed from the Indian Ocean, the world economy spent most of its history centred on Asia — visible in the direction bullion flowed and in where the world’s goods were made. Integration advanced and retreated in waves shaped by climate, disease, technology and money, along a durable maritime channel and a periodically resurgent overland one. Europe’s dominance was real and transformative but bounded — concentrated in the century after 1750, built partly on the Asian centrality it overturned, and resting at its height on the Indian Ocean periphery it governed. Its timing and depth remain debated, and the East’s aggregate return — measurable but partial, achieved within a Western-built order — is the long arc closing, not simply repeating.

Call this the spine. It treats European dominance as an interruption rather than a destination, but a bounded one, and four qualifications bound it. They recur throughout the book, so carry all four:

  1. the interruption was consequential, roughly two transformative centuries rather than a blip;
  2. its timing and depth are genuinely open, and are argued over in 7  The Great Divergence: the centre moves west;
  3. the eventual return east is partial, being aggregate weight rather than a restoration of per-capita parity; and
  4. it is system-dependent, since the Asian re-rise happened inside a US-anchored order.

We do not settle the spine here. We assemble the tools to test it, and return to weigh it in the Conclusion.

The framework: what makes a place productive

Underneath the whole book sits one piece of economics, stated plainly here because every chapter refers back to it. What a society can produce depends on four things: its useful knowledge, its capital, its people, and its land. Written out, output is a function of technology, capital, labour and nature:

\[Y = f(A, K, L, N)\]

This is a scaffold, not a model to be solved. Nobody in this book estimates it. Its job is to make us ask, once per topic, which of the four inputs was doing the work and which one was in the way.

A is technology and useful knowledge. Not only machines: techniques, crops, navigation, the ability to write a contract that a stranger will honour. Institutions live here too, entered as productivity rather than as a separate category, which is a simplification some economists will dislike and which keeps the framework to four terms.

K is capital assets. Ships, warehouses, irrigation works, tools, pack animals, stock in hand. A produced thing, not consumed at once, yielding services through time. That is the whole of what K means in this book, and keeping it that narrow is deliberate, because three different ideas get folded into it constantly.

Money is a function. It is what an object or a claim does: quote a price, settle a debt, hold value between now and later. Silver by weight does it. So do cowries, coins, banknotes and a bank deposit.

Credit is a relationship. One party holds a claim, another carries the liability, and purchasing power has moved across time. The obligation can be written in silver, in grain, in cattle or in coin, and Mesopotamia was running interest-bearing debt around 3000 BCE, two and a half millennia before anyone struck a coin.

Capital is neither. It is a thing somebody built and can now use.

Money can finance capital and credit can finance capital, and it is tempting to conclude that they are therefore capital. They are not. A society cannot make more because the ship was paid for with borrowed silver rather than saved silver. The ship is the capital.

Follow one lump of metal and the three come apart cleanly. Silver in the ore body is N. Refined and cast into an ingot it is output, and wealth. Used to quote a price or settle an obligation it is performing a monetary function. Promised across time it sits inside a credit relationship. Four positions, one metal, and the metal never changed.

Which is why the tidy version has to be resisted. That version says bullion is a good first and becomes money later, at some date, and it is wrong in a way worth naming: it makes a claim about the calendar when the real claim is about function. Refined silver is always an economic good. In a great many societies it is also, at the same time, doing monetary work. Coinage standardises a monetary object and certifies its content. It does not invent money.

The categories overlap, and that is the point rather than a defect. A bank deposit is money and a credit claim at once. Cattle are capital and a store of wealth. The question is never which box something belongs in. It is which function it is performing, here, in this transaction.

None of which costs us the tracer. Silver remains the best single thing to follow across five and a half thousand years, and 8  The first global economy: integration on colonial terms is where the direction it runs in reverses. What changes there is what the metal is doing, and who will honour a face value at the far end of a voyage. It is not a date on which metal turned into capital.

L is labour and people. In this book that means migration, slavery and indenture at least as often as it means population growth, because for most of the period the way to get more labour into a place was to move it there, and frequently to move it by force.

N is land, energy and resources. Most courses drop N, or fold it into capital, on the reasonable grounds that in a modern economy land is a small share of costs. Before fossil fuel it is not a small share of anything. Energy came from land, as food for people and animals and as wood for heat; materials came from land; and the binding constraint on almost every pre-modern economy was how much land it could reach. Restoring N to the front rank is the single most consequential thing this framework does.

N also has a second face, and the second face is where the economics gets interesting. There is N as nature: soil, rainfall, ore bodies, disease environment, the physical world a society is handed. And there is N as power: what happens when a resource is not merely scarce but concentrated, so that whoever holds the place it comes from can charge for access. Banda nutmeg, Potosi silver, Baltic ship timber, the Straits of Malacca, Suez, the coalfields. Once you see the second face, a great deal of what looks like commercial history turns out to be a fight over N. The armed trading companies of 6  Companies, coercion and the contest of the oceans are the clearest case: they are not merchants who happened to carry cannon, they are organisations built to hold chokepoints.3

3 Sources: the framework follows the module’s own governing-framework document; on chokepoints and armed trade, 6  Companies, coercion and the contest of the oceans. Read more: Findlay and O’Rourke, Power and Plenty (2007), for the same argument made at book length.

Two long-run patterns fall straight out of the four terms, which is the main reason for having them.

The first is Malthus, restated. With N roughly fixed and A improving slowly, any gain in output gets absorbed by more people rather than by richer people. That is not a claim about human nature; it is arithmetic on a constrained input. It is why almost every flourishing economy in the first six chapters of this book flourishes by getting bigger, and why reading that as rising living standards is the commonest mistake a student makes here.

The second is the escape, around 1800, and it is the same sentence run backwards. A surges, because useful knowledge becomes cumulative and fossil-fuelled, and simultaneously the N constraint relaxes, because coal substitutes for land as a source of energy and colonial acreage substitutes for domestic acreage as a source of food and fibre. Both terms move at once, and only then does A finally outrun L. 7  The Great Divergence: the centre moves west is that story, and the framework is why the chapter can treat coal and colonies as two versions of the same relief rather than as two unrelated advantages.4

4 Sources: Wrigley, Energy and the English Industrial Revolution (2010), on the organic-economy constraint; Pomeranz, The Great Divergence (2000), on ghost acreage. Read more: 7  The Great Divergence: the centre moves west.

One sentence ties the framework to everything else in the book, and it is the sentence to memorise if you memorise one:

The production function tells you what a society could produce; demand decides whether it does; and control of N decides who keeps the gains.

Three separate claims, and the second and third are the ones usually left out. Capacity is the supply side, and it is what the function describes. Demand is whether that capacity is used at all, which is not automatic: European households had to acquire a taste for cotton, sugar and tea before Asian and Atlantic production had a market to grow into, and Chapter 2 contains the only pre-modern evidence in this book that anyone actually ate the pepper they imported. And power is who captures the output once it exists, which is where the bottleneck-holder, the armed company and the colonial state come in. A history that runs only on capacity ends up explaining why Britain could industrialise and never explaining why India, which could also have done, did not.

Demand deserves a further sentence, because it is the term this kind of history most often drops. It is easy to write a story in which Asia could produce and Europe could not, and to stop there. But production without a buyer is not economic activity, it is a warehouse.

Some of the largest movements in this book are movements in what people wanted rather than in what could be made: the European acquisition of a taste for sweetened tea, which reorganised agriculture on three continents; the shift of pepper from a ritual and medical substance to a culinary one, which changed the volume required without changing the supply chain at all; the Chinese demand for silver as a tax medium, which is why American mines mattered to a country that never traded with the Americas.

When a chapter cannot explain why a flow started, the missing term is usually this one.

In practice the framework is used one line at a time. At the end of every chapter there is an input ledger: which of the four inputs was doing the work in this period, and which one was binding?

The answers are not always the ones you would guess. In 1  Deep structures: a world without a centre the binding input is N, but not in the way the phrase suggests, because the constraint on the Indus cities turns out to be the variability of the water rather than its quantity. In 6  Companies, coercion and the contest of the oceans the input that moves is K, since what the chartered company supplies is not better technology or cheaper labour but a way of assembling capital and violence in the same institution. By 10  The Asian rebalancing: the arc comes home the binding input has become A, and the argument about whether it will stay that way is the argument about the next fifty years.

Ten lines, one per chapter. By the end you will have written the book’s argument yourselves.

The four questions, and where they meet the framework

The book asks the same four questions of every period. They organise every chapter and they are the method the book hands you.

ImportantThe four motivating questions
  • Q1 — Direction. Is the system integrating or disintegrating? Two refinements the evidence forces on the simple cycle: integration breeds fragility, since the links that carry goods carry shocks; and disintegration has winners, since a fracturing core can loosen its grip on a periphery.
  • Q2 — Channels. Which routes carry the integrating weight? Three run through this book: the maritime Indian Ocean, the desert-and-oasis corridor, and the steppe. The ocean is the durable artery; the overland channels had one true climax, the Mongol Pax of 4  The high-medieval boom and the Mongol climax.
  • Q3 — Modes. What crosses the connection? The answer is read off the framework, input by input. The real flows are output itself, useful knowledge, people and capital assets — Y, A, L and K. Alongside them run monetary and financial flows: settlement assets, and claims and liabilities. Bullion can sit on both sides at once, which is why its direction serves as the book’s running tracer of the centre of gravity.
  • Q4 — Europe. What is Europe’s role: peripheral, entrant, dominant, one of several cores? And how does it change? Asked as an open question, not assumed.

Q4 is the odd one out and readers are entitled to ask why Europe gets a question to itself. The answer is not that Europe is more important than the other actors; for most of this book it is plainly less so. It is that Europe is where the reader’s prior sits. Every student arriving at this module has absorbed a version of the story in which European development is the main sequence and everything else is background. The way to test a prior is to make it a question rather than leave it as an assumption.

So Q4 asks in each period what Europe’s role actually was, with the possible answers laid out in advance and none of them privileged: peripheral, entrant, one core among several, dominant. Asked that way, the answer changes across the ten chapters in a pattern that is genuinely interesting, and in 1  Deep structures: a world without a centre it changes twice, since Bronze Age Europe turns out to be a resource periphery supplying tin to an Asian-centred system, which is a much stranger finding than either “central” or “absent”.

The four questions and the production function are not two separate pieces of apparatus. They are two axes of the same one. The production function is vertical: it asks what makes a place productive. The four questions are horizontal: they ask how places connect. And they meet at N.

That gives us a definition of integration with some economics in it. To integrate is to relax your own binding constraint by drawing on somebody else’s inputs: importing land-intensive goods because you have run out of land, monetising foreign silver because your own mines are poor, importing labour because you cannot raise enough of it, borrowing technology because you did not invent it. Q3’s four flows are the four inputs. Goods are N embodied, in the sense that a cargo of grain or tin is somebody else’s land arriving in usable form. Technology is A. People are L. Capital is K. The mapping is close enough to be useful and loose enough that you should check it in each period rather than assume it.

Where the centre of gravity sat, and how sure we are

The spine is a claim about where the weight of the world economy sat, and a claim like that is only as good as its definition of weight.

Weight is output. The centre of gravity is the point world economic activity balances around, and the quantity being balanced is production. Not territory, not armies, and not population for its own sake.

Not all output counts equally. What the spine is about is the connected economy: output that trade enabled, or that was produced for trade, and above all for long-distance trade. A subsistence farmer’s grain is output, and in aggregate it dwarfs everything else in every chapter before the nineteenth century, but it never moved and it cannot move a centre of gravity. So the weighting favours the goods that travelled, the workshops that made them and the ports that handled them. This is why a chapter can call Rome the largest single economy of its age and still put the centre of gravity east of it. The two statements measure different things and both are true.

That choice needs defending rather than asserting, because there is an obvious objection and it is a good one. The connected economy was small: in the periods this book opens with, the long-distance trade is on the order of a few per cent of the output of the economies doing it. Weight the small connected part and disregard the enormous subsistence part, and you have built a measure that locates the centre wherever the tradeable goods were made. Since the tradeable goods were largely made in Asia, Asia cannot lose. A reader entitled to be suspicious will say the measure was chosen to produce the answer.

The reply is that the two measures answer different questions, and this book is asking the second one. Total output answers where was most production happening, which before 1800 is very nearly where did most people live. That is a real fact and a large one, but it tells you nothing about who could set a price. Connected output answers whose goods did other people reorganise themselves to obtain, and on whose terms. That question has testable consequences, which is what makes it worth asking: it predicts where metal accumulated, which is physically observable in hoards and ice cores; and it predicts who could refuse a deal. Chapter 2 catches the second one happening, when Indian merchants stopped accepting Roman silver at the moment its content fell.

Two honesties follow. For much of the arc the two measures agree, because population and production moved together, so the choice is doing less work than the objection implies. But they come apart exactly where the argument is most contested, and the book will say which one it is using at those points. And the measure is not neutral about what counts as economic weight: it treats the ability to set terms as the thing worth measuring. That is a position, and a reader who thinks aggregate production is the better question will read the same evidence and reach a different verdict. They are entitled to.

Before about 1800, output is read through population, and the circularity has to be said out loud. In a Malthusian world more food meant more people rather than richer people, so a region’s share of world output tracked its share of world population fairly closely, which makes population a defensible proxy. But most historical output estimates for the pre-modern world were themselves built from population figures and assumed income levels. When the two series agree before 1800, part of that agreement is a fact about the world and part of it is an artefact of how the numbers were made. A good student will spot this, and it is much better coming from us.

Bullion indicates; it does not define. The direction metal flowed is this book’s running tracer, because metal moved toward the workshop. It is evidence for where the centre lay, and good evidence. It is not what the centre is.

Every verdict is scored, on a four-point scale plus a null. High means several instruments with different biases give one answer. Moderate means the reading is supported, but one instrument is carrying it. Low means it is a reasonable reading and a different one would not be unreasonable. And not applicable means the question has no meaning in this period yet, which is an honest answer rather than a dodge, and is the entry for 1  Deep structures: a world without a centre.

Direction and magnitude are scored separately, because they are separate claims and the evidence for them is usually of different quality. Chapter 2 is the standing example: that Roman metal moved east is about as secure as anything in ancient economic history, and how much of it moved is contested by two orders of magnitude. An answer that gives one confidence level for both has run two claims together.

Applying that scale across all ten verdicts turns up something worth knowing before you start. This book knows direction far better than it knows magnitude. Of the nine periods where the question has a subject, seven score high on direction and five score low on scale. We can say with some confidence which way the weight sat and which way it was moving, in almost every period. We can rarely say by how much. That asymmetry is not a failing of this particular book; it is a property of the evidence, since the instruments that establish direction, such as the way metal flowed or what a place exported against what it imported, are robust to being wrong about quantities by a factor of two. It does mean that a reader who wants numbers will be more often disappointed than a reader who wants an argument, and it is why Appendix C — The centre of gravity, chapter by chapter now scores the two separately.

Two instruments, not one. For nine chapters the centre has to be inferred, from city sizes, trade composition, the direction of bullion, shares of world manufacturing. In 10  The Asian rebalancing: the arc comes home it can also be computed: Danny Quah’s economic centre of gravity, the distance-weighted average location of world output. That calculation is offered there as a complement to the reading this book has been making all along, not as a replacement. It weights all output equally, where this book weights connected output, so it answers a slightly different question, and two differently-derived readings that agree are worth more than either alone.

One switch, announced in advance. Chapters 1 to 6 score aggregate weight; from 7  The Great Divergence: the centre moves west the measure that matters becomes income per head. That switch is the Great Divergence, and the chapter says so. But it does mean the early and late verdicts are not marks on the same ruler, and Appendix C — The centre of gravity, chapter by chapter keeps them in one table precisely so that the seam is visible.

The bullion tracer, and why it flips

Before modern capital markets, metal moved toward the workshop. People who made things the rest of the world wanted, Indian cotton and Chinese silk and porcelain, were paid in silver and gold, which pooled where production was most valued. So in antiquity Roman silver drained east to India (2  The first world system: Rome was the customer, Asia the workshop), and in the early-modern centuries New World silver flowed east to China (5  The world becomes one — and stays Asian). The tracer even works in reverse where it should: the Abbasid core paid silver north to its slave-and-fur periphery (3  Maritime apogee: Baghdad and Guangzhou, not Aachen), mirroring Rome. That reversal matters, because a tracer that only ever points one way is a rule rather than an instrument.

One complication belongs here rather than in a footnote, because it arrives before the pattern does. The earliest traceable silver flow in this book runs the wrong way. In the third and second millennia BCE, the metal moves west to east into Mesopotamia rather than east toward a workshop, because at that date the workshop in the later sense does not exist and silver is moving toward demand rather than toward production. So the tracer that organises eight chapters is complicated by the first one. That is not a reason to drop it. It is a reason to state what the tracer assumes: it works when one region is making things another region wants badly enough to pay in metal for, and before that condition holds, metal moves for other reasons entirely.

After the nineteenth century the tracer flips, and what flips is not the obvious thing.

The metal is doing something new. Under the classical gold standard, gold is not merely accepted by weight at the far end of a voyage; it anchors a settlement system that reaches across borders, and claims denominated in it circulate on the credit of institutions rather than on assay. Follow it then and it leads to the financial core, London and afterwards New York, rather than to the workshop.

Note who underwrote that core. India’s colonial drain helped finance Britain’s payments deficits (8  The first global economy: integration on colonial terms), sourced in the periphery and intermediated by the centre.

Integration breeds fragility

Q1’s first refinement earns a paragraph of its own, because it is the closest thing this book has to a general finding. The links that carry goods carry shocks. A network that moves pepper efficiently moves pathogens efficiently, and a system whose parts have specialised in what they do best is a system in which no part can feed itself when the connections fail.

The pattern shows up at every scale. The Late Bronze Age palaces fell more or less together, and the tin that made their bronze came from far enough away that a single interruption reached all of them. The Antonine plague travelled the same routes as the eastern trade and struck both ends of Eurasia within a few years. The Black Death moved along the Mongol roads that had just made overland trade viable for the first time in centuries. The financial crisis of the 1930s transmitted through exactly the capital-market linkages that had been the previous generation’s achievement.

The lesson is not that integration is bad, which would be a strange thing to conclude from a book about it. It is that integration and fragility are the same property observed at different moments, and that a period’s prosperity and its vulnerability are usually built out of the same links. When a chapter reports a system at its peak, the useful next question is what that peak has made it unable to survive.

The two yardsticks

The book measures two different things and keeps them apart, and half the disagreements in this field are two people scoring on different instruments without noticing.

Integration is measured two ways, because the evidence changes. Before there are prices, it is read from the diffusion of goods, weights and standards: Indus seals turning up in Mesopotamia, a chariot design reaching Shang China. That establishes that two economies touched, and says almost nothing about how much, how often, or at what price. Once there is coinage and quoted prices, integration can be read from price convergence instead, which is a far stronger instrument. Two instruments, one concept, and the switch happens around 3  Maritime apogee: Baghdad and Guangzhou, not Aachen.

Success is the other yardstick, and it has two settings. Quantity, or extensive growth, means more people, bigger cities, larger total output: prosperity turned into numbers. Quality, or intensive growth, means sustained income per head, which is the modern escape and, on this timescale, a very recent one. Almost every thriving economy in this book is thriving on the first setting. Han China, Song China, Mughal India, Qing China: all much larger than their contemporaries, none of them richer per person in the way the phrase implies today.

So say which yardstick you are using. An answer that says China was richer than Europe in 1700 without saying whether it means in aggregate or per head has not yet said anything.

Most of the economy was never priced

One more piece of arithmetic before the evidence, and it is the one most likely to be forgotten. For almost all of the period this book covers, most of what was produced was never bought or sold.

Think of output as three layers. The bottom and by far the largest is subsistence and obligation: what a household grew and ate, plus what it handed over as rent, tribute, tithe or labour dues. That second category has no clean modern counterpart, and it matters, because a peasant handing over a third of the harvest is transferring real output without any price being formed anywhere. Above that sits regional exchange, in local markets, at prices that were real but seldom recorded. Above that, thinnest of all, sits long-distance trade.

Our evidence comes overwhelmingly from the top layer, because that is the layer that generated records: customs registers, merchant letters, tax farms, port dues. So when a source gives us a number, the first question is always what the number is a number of. A figure for the value of the eastern trade is not a figure for the Roman economy, and treating one as a share of the other requires knowing how much of the economy was never in the accounts at all.

Confuse the layers and it goes badly. Chapter 2 has to ask how large the Roman eastern trade was, and the published estimates of the annual value of Rome’s Indian Ocean imports run from about 920 million sesterces to about 17,640 million. The high end is roughly the size of the most-cited estimate of the entire Roman economy. Nobody involved is careless: the estimates differ because each takes the tax assessment of one documented cargo and multiplies it by an assumed number of ships, and the assumptions differ. But notice what has happened. A number drawn entirely from the thin top layer, the customs record, is being compared with a number for all three layers at once, and the comparison only works if you know what fraction of Roman output ever passed through a market at all. It did not, and we do not.

Trade costs, and what price convergence cannot see

The strongest instrument in the book is price convergence, and what it does is narrower than its reputation. The test is not that a good costs the same in two places, because it never does. The test is that the gap between the two prices is bounded by the cost of moving the good, and narrows as that cost falls. A stable price gap between two trading places is therefore not a market failing to integrate. It is the cost of integration, quoted.

Put slightly more formally, trade settles where the gap the market will bear equals the trade cost: freight, plus tolls, plus risk, plus whatever has to be paid for protection. That last term is why integration in this book is as much a political variable as a technological one. And it means a narrowing gap can arise three ways. Demand can rise where the good is bought; supply can rise where it is sold; or the cost of carriage can fall. Only the third is integration. So the sentence to carry into the exam is that rising volumes are not evidence of integration. Converging prices are.5

5 Sources: Shiue and Keller, “Markets in China and Europe on the eve of the Industrial Revolution”, American Economic Review 97 (2007), for the method applied comparatively. Read more: 6  Companies, coercion and the contest of the oceans and 8  The first global economy: integration on colonial terms, where the test does most of its work.

The instrument also has a blind spot worth knowing, because it is exactly the blind spot that matters in the early chapters. A good arriving somewhere for the first time has no prior price to converge on. A chariot reaching Shang China, or the potato reaching Ireland, is integration that this test cannot see at all. Before roughly 500 BCE we are working almost entirely in the blind spot, which is why 1  Deep structures: a world without a centre has to lean on diffusion and says so.

1  Deep structures: a world without a centre does, though, contain one place where the test can almost be run, and it shows what the instrument feels like when it works. In the Old Assyrian trade of the early second millennium BCE, tin bought at Assur cost about 6.7 shekels of silver per hundred shekels of tin, and the same tin sold in Anatolia at about 14.3. That is a gap of roughly 2.1 times across about a thousand kilometres of donkey caravan. It is not arbitrage, because arbitrage is riskless and simultaneous and this was neither: the merchant financed the goods, waited a hundred days, and bore the risk of losing everything to bandits or a change of ruler. The gap is the cost of carriage, quoted, in a world with no coinage and no market in the modern sense. And the gap did not close. It persisted for generations, which tells you that the costs were structural rather than a temporary friction somebody was about to compete away.

The kinds of evidence this book is built from

Every period in this book is known through a different instrument, and the instrument decides what can be claimed. A chapter resting on ice cores can say things a chapter resting on customs registers cannot, and vice versa, and neither can say what a chapter resting on a national accounts series can. Most of the mistakes students make in this field are not mistakes about history. They are claims made at a level of precision the instrument does not support.

The production function gives that survey its shape, because each input has its own evidence base and its own characteristic way of going wrong.

N: land, climate and resources

The evidence for N is mostly physical and mostly not written by anybody. Ice cores from Greenland and the Alps record atmospheric deposition year by year, so a layer of lead in the ice is a by-product of somebody smelting silver out of galena a continent away. Speleothems, which is to say stalagmites, record rainfall in their oxygen isotopes. Lake sediments, pollen cores, marine cores off river deltas and tree rings all give climate at various resolutions. Ore bodies have chemical fingerprints, so a metal object can sometimes be traced to the ground it came out of.

The characteristic failure is dating, and the second is scale. A stalagmite might be dated to within a century, which sounds precise until you need to know whether a drought preceded a civilisation’s collapse or followed it. Chapter 1 contains the cleanest instance in the book: three independent archives agree that the climate around 2200 BCE got drier, and their error bands are each as wide as the three-century gap they are being asked to bridge, so none of them can order the climate event against the settlement change it supposedly caused. Synchrony is not sequence, and without sequence there is no causation. The scale problem is related. A core measures its own catchment, not the world, and a Greenland record weighted toward north-west Europe should not be read as a statement about global production.

L: people

Population is reconstructed from settlement survey, from cemetery demography, from skeletal and stature evidence, and increasingly from ancient DNA. Isotopes in tooth enamel give something different again: strontium and oxygen laid down in childhood record where a person grew up, and enamel never remodels, so the molars carry an ordered record of where somebody was at particular ages.

The characteristic failure of population evidence is worth stating as a rule, because it recurs. Almost every population figure in this book is an area multiplied by an assumed number of people per unit of area, and the assumed coefficient does most of the work. Change the coefficient and the answer changes proportionally; change your mind about which line on the ground counts as the edge of the settlement and it changes by more than that. Appendix D — How the numbers were made sets out two worked cases: an urbanisation rate for Sumer where two careful scholars differ by a factor of three, and a population for Pataliputra where the published estimates span a factor of about thirteen without anybody disputing any evidence at all.

Isotopes and DNA look like two ways of measuring the same thing and are not. Isotopes measure residence; DNA measures descent. A merchant who sailed to Carthage, traded for a season and sailed home is visible to the first instrument and completely invisible to the second, because he left no descendants there. His grandchild, born in Carthage to a local mother, is the exact reverse. This is why the finding that western Mediterranean Punic populations carry almost no Levantine ancestry shows that Phoenician settlers did not dominate the gene pool. It does not show that Phoenicians did not go there.

Both instruments report lower bounds, and where they disagree the disagreement is itself a measurement: it is the ratio of circulation to settlement, which is a real economic quantity and one we would otherwise have no way to see. It also forces a correction on a sentence people say loosely. Goods and techniques travelled further than settlers. Whether they travelled further than people is a different claim, and usually not the one the evidence supports.6

Read more: 2  The first world system: Rome was the customer, Asia the workshop and 3  Maritime apogee: Baghdad and Guangzhou, not Aachen, where both instruments are used.

K: capital, money and credit

One heading covers three things the framework keeps apart, and the reason is the archives rather than the economics. Hoards, stray finds, die studies and bullion shipment records are evidence about money. Loan tablets and partnership contracts are evidence about credit. Ship timbers, irrigation surveys and warehouse accounts are evidence about capital itself. In practice they arrive in the same box, because the people who wrote down one of them wrote down the others on the same tablet.

So the evidence for all three is hoards, stray coin finds, mint output estimated from die studies, bullion shipment records where a state bothered to keep them, and above all merchant archives: the letters, loans and accounts of people doing business. Keep the categories separate when reading them. Coin tells you about settlement; it does not tell you how many ships there were.

Its characteristic failure is that archives survive by accident, and disproportionately by catastrophe. Mesopotamia’s cuneiform tablets were unbaked clay, and what fired them hard enough to last four thousand years was the burning of the palaces that held them. We read those economies in detail because they were destroyed. A place that was never sacked, or whose records were kept on palm leaf or paper in a wet climate, is not less commercial. It is less legible. Coin hoards carry their own version of the same problem: a hoard dates when somebody decided to bury money and did not come back for it, which is a fact about insecurity as much as about circulation, and which can post-date the arrival of the coins by a generation.

There is a second and subtler difficulty with monetary evidence, which is that coins are recycled. The metal in a coin struck in 200 CE may have been mined in 50 BCE, melted down twice and alloyed with something else on the way. So a chemical analysis of coinage tells you what was in the melting pot, not what came out of the ground, and the two questions have different answers. Chapter 2 puts an ice core and a coin series side by side for exactly this reason: the ice records what was smelted, and the coins record what was refined and recycled. They look like two measurements of one thing and they are not.

A: technology and technique

A is read from the objects themselves, and from the sciences that interrogate them: archaeometallurgy, lead and tin isotope provenancing, residue analysis, and radiocarbon dating of the contexts things are found in.

Its characteristic failure runs the opposite way to the others, and it is a cheerful one. Better dating destroys more claims than it creates. When a technique improves, the usual result is that something long believed turns out to rest on a specimen that is intrusive, or younger than its context, or misidentified. Chapter 1 carries the cleanest example: domestic chickens were for decades said to originate in the Indus Valley, and a systematic re-dating programme found that every European Neolithic and Bronze Age specimen tested was intrusive, moving the earliest secure domestic chickens to Thailand and about a thousand years later. Nothing was fabricated and nobody was careless. The instrument got sharper and a claim fell over.

That is worth holding onto as a disposition rather than a fact, because it tells you how to read the confident sentences in older books, including good ones. A claim about origins that was established before radiocarbon calibration, or before isotope provenancing, or before it was possible to sequence a genome from a tooth, has not been refuted. It has simply never been tested by the instrument that could test it. Some of those claims will survive. The pattern of the last twenty years is that most do not.

What each proxy can and cannot support

Set the limits out in one place, because most over-claiming in this field is one of these four moves.

What the standard proxies will and will not bear.
Proxy What it supports What it does not
Diffusion of goods Contact, and the geographic range of a network Volume, frequency, price, or who profited
Direction of carriage Relative shipping cost; which end wanted the other’s goods How the surplus was divided between the two ends
Population Aggregate scale, under a Malthusian assumption Income per head, ever
Settlement counts An upper bound on decline The decline itself, since the record-keeping collapses too

The fourth row is the subtlest and the most often missed. When a state falls apart, the number of attested settlements drops. But the thing that produced the attestations, an administration writing things down, fell apart at the same time. So a fall in recorded settlements is a composite of real decline and lost visibility, and it gives you a ceiling on the first rather than a measurement of it.

Absence of data is not absence of activity

What survives reflects record-keeping conventions and the accidents of climate and preservation, not economic weight. Organic records rot in the humid monsoon tropics and survive in cool dry Europe and arid Egypt, so the surviving record systematically under-documents the ocean world this book is written around.

Because the direction of that bias is known, it licenses exactly one move: treat every figure we have as a lower bound. That is a real licence and a useful one. It supports claims about direction and it supports the statement that a trade was at least as large as the record shows.

It licenses nothing else, and in particular it does not license filling the gap with a conclusion. A student who writes “the evidence is biased against Asia, therefore Asian trade was much larger” has done exactly what the monsoon headline did: taken a real tendency and converted it into a specific claim the tendency cannot carry. The bias tells you which way to lean. It does not tell you how far.

The evidence map is the inverse of the production map

One commodity demonstrates the whole problem, and it is the commodity this book returns to most often. Black pepper is south Indian, and every Roman-period peppercorn ever recovered survives in one of exactly three ways. Desiccated, in the hyper-arid Egyptian desert at Berenike and Myos Hormos. Waterlogged, in anaerobic mud in Germany, Britain and France. Or mineralised, chemically replaced in cesspit fill in Campania. Too dry for microbes, too wet for oxygen, or turned to stone.

The monsoon Indian Ocean littoral, where the pepper was actually grown, offers none of the three. Warm, wet and oxygenated is the worst possible combination for preservation. So the finds are thousands of peppercorns from Egypt, dozens from north-west Europe, and until very recently essentially none from the producing region. Read that distribution as a trade map and you would conclude that pepper was an Egyptian and European commodity.

The transferable version is this: the map of the evidence is not the map of the economy, it is the map of the preservation conditions. Ask yourself which commodities have no preservation window anywhere, and the answer, uncomfortably, is textiles. Cotton and silk rot in every climate that grew them.7

7 Sources: Cobb, on the recovery contexts of Roman-period pepper, citing Cappers (2006), Livarda (2011), and Robinson and Rowan (2015). Read more: 2  The first world system: Rome was the customer, Asia the workshop.

There is a second version of the same problem, and it operates at a scale that is hard to take in. When the ice sheets melted the sea rose roughly 120 metres, and it drowned the continental shelves. Sundaland, the shelf joining Borneo, Java and Sumatra to mainland Asia, was about 4.7 million square kilometres of dry land at the last glacial maximum against about 2.3 million today. Something on the order of two and a third million square kilometres went under water. That is an area about the size of India, and it went under along exactly the kind of coastline where people prefer to live.

Now ask what has been recovered from it. From the whole submerged Sunda shelf the record is some six thousand vertebrate fossils and two fragments of Homo erectus skull, dated to around 140,000 years ago. No artefacts. No structures. No settlements. And even that material was not found by archaeologists: it came up in a commercial dredging operation that removed five million cubic metres of sand to build an artificial island.

This one cuts in a direction students find uncomfortable, so be exact. It does not show that there were drowned cities, and people who want there to have been drowned cities reach for it constantly. It shows something more disciplined and more useful: that an area the size of India has been removed from the evidence base along the most habitable margin of a densely peopled part of the world, and that on that shelf almost nobody has looked. The honest statement is not that there was more down there. It is that for this region in this period the record is not thin because the activity was thin. It is thin because the ground is under a hundred metres of water.

That is the pepper argument again in a different medium, and it is the strongest version of it in the book. Which is exactly why it is the one most often over-driven, and why the closing of the argument is the part to watch. The closing is more interesting than the opening.

Europe is the control case, because there people have looked. A four-year European research network assembled a register of submerged prehistoric finds that now holds about 2,640 records, and the shape of that register is the answer. Around 58 per cent are single finds rather than sites. Only about one in six is an undisturbed occupation layer. Depth is recorded for under a third, and of those nearly nine in ten lie shallower than five metres, with three sites in all of Europe recorded deeper than forty. Denmark alone accounts for two-thirds of the total, which is a fact about Danish research funding rather than about Danish prehistory. And the volume reporting all this says plainly that evidence for settlement or in-situ activity from waters more than twelve nautical miles offshore has yet to be found in the North Sea, or anywhere else in the world.

So the submerged record is real, and substantial, and shallow, fragmentary and forager-shaped. There is no gradient in it pointing toward greater complexity further out.

Keep the positive case in view, though, because otherwise this reads as an argument that nothing can be found under water. Things can. Atlit-Yam, off the Carmel coast in Israel, is a Neolithic village of about nine thousand years ago sitting four hundred metres offshore under eight to twelve metres of water: stone-built structures, burials, and a stone-lined well nearly six metres deep. It is one of more than twenty submerged prehistoric sites known off that one coastline. That is what a drowned settlement looks like when it survives and somebody excavates it, and the thing to notice is how ordinary it is. Its economy is continuous with its contemporaries on land.

Say why the deeper water stays unlooked-at, because the reason is economic rather than intellectual. A single exploratory survey with ship time runs to two or three million dollars, and anything systematic is an order of magnitude more. So the work that does happen is usually piggy-backed on something commercial. When a sonar survey for the Qatar-Bahrain causeway threw up 83 features of possible archaeological interest, divers inspected about half of them, and every one turned out to be modern or natural. That is the honest state of the question: not a search that has failed, and not a search that has succeeded, but a search that has barely been funded.

The chronology closes the question from the other end. Sea level came within about a metre of its present position by roughly five thousand years ago. Uruk reached 250 hectares, and writing appeared, at about 3100 BCE. Before that, Mesopotamian villages were rarely larger than one or two hectares. The shelves therefore finished drowning before the first city existed anywhere, and the whole window during which they flooded runs, in the words of the field’s own flagship volume, from mobile hunter-gatherers with no pottery, no agriculture and no metal tools at one end to small towns at the other. Both ends of that window are on dry land, in the record, where we can see them.

Which gives the general form of the move, and you will need it more than once. A preservation gap licenses “we cannot see what was there”. It never licenses “what was there was X”. Getting to X needs positive evidence, and where X is a civilisation the positive evidence is not confined to the drowned strip: cities need quarries, farmland, hinterlands, refuse and trade goods, none of which stops politely at the old shoreline. The step from “we cannot see” to “therefore something particular was there” is the step this section exists to refuse.89

The oldest annual series in the world are tax records

The longest continuous annual measurements we have of anything physical were not made by anybody interested in the physical world. The Nile flood runs annually from 622 CE because flood height set the Egyptian land tax. The Beaune grape harvest runs from 1354 because the town council had to pay the pickers on a date. Lake Suwa’s freeze runs from 1443 because a shrine read the ice for an omen.

Nobody was measuring the climate. They were measuring a tax base, a wage bill, an omen and a season, and climate is the by-product. Which means the geography of the pre-modern climate record is a map of fiscal and institutional capacity, and it degrades when the polity degrades. The Nilometer holds between seventy-seven and a hundred readings per century up to 1470, and then thirty-two in the sixteenth century and thirty-one in the seventeenth. The density of a series is itself a datum about the state that produced it.

Two cautions on that particular series, since it recurs in this book. The apparent upward drift after about 1300 is the river bed silting up and raising the gauge datum, not a wetter Nile. And only the annual maximum is the tax-relevant variable, so it is the only one that was recorded with consistent care.10

Claims carry grades, and a passed check is not a verified fact

Because the evidence is so uneven, this book grades individual claims as well as verdicts. A claim is secure when a named source has been read and says it. It is probable when it takes one step of inference, or rests on a synthesis rather than the original study. It is contested when specialists disagree in print, in which case the disagreement is taught rather than resolved away. And it is single-source when one authority stands behind it and nobody has independently confirmed it, which is a warning rather than a criticism.

One exercise shows what that grading finds. In preparing 2  The first world system: Rome was the customer, Asia the workshop this book compiled a register of claims about early historic South Asia. On the forty-five entries whose subject is genuinely South Asian, fifteen and a half per cent came out secure and twenty-nine per cent contested. Fewer than one claim in six about the Mauryan and post-Mauryan economy is secure on the book’s own grading. That ratio is itself a finding, and a more useful one than most of the individual claims, because it tells you how to read every confident sentence written about the period, including the ones in this book.

A recent case shows what the grading is for, and it is still live. In 2023 a team published a study of Gunung Padang in West Java arguing that the hill is not a hill but a construction, begun between 25,000 and 14,000 BCE, and “potentially the oldest pyramid in the world”. The journal retracted it in March 2024, and the reason given is this section’s whole argument in one sentence: the radiocarbon dating “was applied to soil samples that were not associated with any artifacts or features that could be reliably interpreted as anthropogenic”.

Notice what is and is not wrong there. The dates are probably fine and the laboratory work looks careful. What failed is the join between the number and the claim. The age belongs to the soil; the construction event was inferred separately, from geophysics, and then had the soil’s age attached to it. Ask of any number in this book what it is a number of, and you will catch that class of error before it catches you. The authors still disagree with the retraction, so the case is still open.11

There is a last rule, and it is the one that cost this book the most to learn. A passed check is not a verified fact. An earlier version of Chapter 1 was fact-checked, and the check certified as correct a claim about the Uluburun shipwreck’s cargo that was false. It passed because the check compared the chapter against the documents then to hand, and the claim had been inherited from one of them rather than tested against the wreck’s excavation report. Agreement between two documents that share an ancestor is not evidence. It is an echo. When you are checking a number, the question is never whether it matches what else you have read. It is whether anybody ever measured it.

The instruments improve, and that is itself the shape of the book

Set the four evidence bases against the chapters and a progression appears which is worth stating once, because it governs what each half of the book is able to argue.

For 1  Deep structures: a world without a centre there are no prices at all. Not few: none. Integration has to be read from the diffusion of objects and standards, and the verdict on the centre of gravity is therefore not applicable. From 2  The first world system: Rome was the customer, Asia the workshop there are coins, quoted prices in a handful of documents, and the first physical output series, so direction can be argued and magnitude cannot. From 3  Maritime apogee: Baghdad and Guangzhou, not Aachen to 6  Companies, coercion and the contest of the oceans the merchant archives thicken and price series begin to run continuously in a few places, which is enough to test convergence over decades. From 7  The Great Divergence: the centre moves west there are wages, prices and the first constructed national accounts, and the argument shifts from where the weight sat to why it moved. By 9  Disintegration: war, depression, and the colonial world and 10  The Asian rebalancing: the arc comes home there are trade statistics collected by states for their own purposes, and finally a centre of gravity that can be computed rather than inferred.

Two things follow. The first is that the book’s later verdicts are better evidenced than its earlier ones, which is simply true and is stated in Appendix C — The centre of gravity, chapter by chapter chapter by chapter. The second is the one people forget: better evidence does not make the later arguments less contested. 7  The Great Divergence: the centre moves west has more data behind it than any chapter before it and contains the book’s least settled question. The quantity of evidence and the level of disagreement are close to independent of one another, and assuming otherwise is how a reader ends up trusting a well-measured claim more than it deserves.

One cargo, and what four questions can do with it

It helps to see the apparatus run once on a real object before the chapters start, and there is a good one to hand. In the late fourteenth century BCE a ship went down off Uluburun, near Kas on the southern Turkish coast, and it was found in 1982. It is the richest single deposit of Bronze Age trade goods ever recovered, and it is a dataset of one.

What was aboard: about ten tonnes of copper, as 354 four-cornered “oxhide” ingots and 121 bun-shaped ones, and roughly a tonne of tin. That is close to the ten-to-one ratio bronze needs, so the cargo is about eleven tonnes of alloy in kit form. Also about 175 glass ingots, around a tonne of terebinth resin in at least 149 Canaanite jars, 135 Cypriot vessels, African ebony, hippopotamus and elephant ivory, Baltic amber and Mesopotamian bitumen. Products of at least nine or ten cultures, in one hull.

Now run the questions. Q2, channels, it answers well: this is a maritime cargo moving counter-clockwise around the eastern Mediterranean, and the amber alone establishes a link running to the Baltic. Q3, modes, it answers partly: the flow here is overwhelmingly goods, which is N embodied, and the presence of merchants’ pan-balance weight sets on the Syrian shekel of about 9.3 grams suggests private commercial calculation rather than pure gift exchange. Q1, direction, it barely touches, because one wreck is a point and direction requires a series. And Q4, Europe, it raises without settling, because where the tin came from is precisely what two laboratories now disagree about.

Then ask what it cannot tell you, which is the more useful half. It cannot tell you how many such ships sailed, or how often, or what any of it cost, or who profited. It cannot even settle whether this was commerce or diplomacy: royal shipments in the Amarna letters run to a hundred or two hundred talents of copper, and 354 oxhide ingots is about 325 talents, which is the same order of magnitude. And there is a preservation trap sitting inside it. The 135 Cypriot vessels aboard are roughly twice the number known from the whole Aegean for the entire Late Bronze Age. If one wreck can double the record, then the record was never a sample of the trade. It is a sample of what happened not to be lost.12

Three ledgers to keep

Ten topics can be ten surveys or one argument, and the difference is whether you write things down as you go. Keep three running lists, one line per chapter.

The centre-of-gravity ledger: where is the weight, is it moving, and how confident are we, on the four-point scale above. The input ledger: which of A, K, L and N is doing the work, and which is binding. And the natural-system beat: the climate, energy or disease force in play, which is there to stop the physical world becoming background scenery.

Three short rows a week. By 10  The Asian rebalancing: the arc comes home you will have assembled the book’s argument in your own handwriting, which is a considerably better position to argue with it from than having read it.

  3. Follow the silver. The early-modern form of the bullion tracer. The same American silver met different monetary conditions and had different fates: Asia absorbed and specialised, the Ottomans inflated, West Africa debased and depleted, and Europe converted a monetary loss into armed-trade and financial power. That last fate is the bridge to the divergence.

The economy sits inside a physical system

Two of the four forces behind Q1 are natural, and that follows from the framework rather than sitting beside it. If N is a first-class input, then climate, energy and disease are not context. They enter the production function directly, as the quantity and reliability of what the land yields and as the number of people available to work it.

This cuts both ways, and the second way is the one that needs guarding. It licenses taking physical shocks seriously as economic events: a volcanic eruption that lowers a Nile flood is a fiscal event, and a pandemic that removes a third of a labour force is a change in L with a century of consequences. But it does not license reading every economic change as a physical one. The test that keeps the two apart is simple and it is the most portable instrument in this book. Geography explains levels; only things that vary can explain timing. A mountain range or a prevailing wind can tell you why a place was persistently rich or persistently isolated. It can never tell you why something happened in one century rather than another, because it did not change. Climate, by contrast, does vary, which is exactly what makes it admissible where geography is not, and exactly why it has to be dated precisely enough to bear the weight.

A note on method

Two commitments follow, and they are why the subtitle is data and debates.

  • Data. The book leans on numbers, because numbers are how you adjudicate a claim like the spine rather than assert it. Figures and tables are reproducible, generated from datasets you can download and re-run. But the data thin out sharply as you go back, and they are biased toward the literate, fiscal, European end of the record. A chapter on the Bronze Age and a chapter on the twentieth century are not equally evidenced, and the book says which is which.
  • Debates. Where the scholarship is genuinely contested, and the standing examples are when the Great Divergence began and why, whether interwar disintegration was man-made or inevitable, and when globalisation really started, the book presents the disagreement rather than resolving it out of sight. Disputed figures are flagged and taught, not silently settled.

Be honest, too, about where this book stands in its own field, because the complaint it is built on is not its invention. David Northrup observes that textbooks “almost invariably” break their coverage at 1500, and Merry Wiesner-Hanks, introducing the Cambridge World History, names the problem in her own preface while conceding that four of that series’ nine books are given to 1400 and after. Northrup pushes further back than 1500, and in a form that reaches this book’s own starting line: the deeper exclusion is the convention of beginning with Sumer and with writing, which removes something between ninety-five and ninety-nine per cent of the human past before the story starts.

There are honest defences of that imbalance and readers should have them. David Christian argues the demographic case, that perhaps eighty per cent of all human lives were lived between the Palaeolithic and 1750, which is a real argument for weighting the deep past. The counter is that evidence, not lives, is what a historian can work with, and evidence accumulates toward the present. And one chapter in the same volume goes after this book’s method rather than its coverage: Michael Lang argues that periodising by chronology, which is exactly what the ten chapters ahead do, smuggles in assumptions about what changes and when. That objection is not answered here. It is worth carrying while you read.13

One last thing, and it is what the subtitle is for. This book states a claim and then spends ten chapters testing it, which means it is possible to read the evidence assembled here and reach a different verdict. That is the intended use. The places where you are most likely to end up disagreeing are marked: the chapters that rest on a single instrument, the verdicts scored low on scale, the disputes set out in Appendix E — The disputes in full, and the derivations in Appendix D — How the numbers were made where a headline figure turns out to be a coefficient multiplied by an area. If you finish the book convinced that European dominance was longer, or deeper, or earlier than the spine allows, the useful question is which of those places you would have to attack to make that case. A claim you cannot say how to falsify is a creed, and the point of setting the argument out this explicitly is that this one is not.

NoteHow to read the chapters

Each chapter opens by following one object that carries its period’s story, locates the period on the arc, sets out the cast and how we know, narrates the period on its own terms, then reads it through the four questions, delivers a centre-of-gravity verdict with a confidence level, and hands off to the next. Look out for the recurring Data exhibit and Debate, and the Follow the money and How we know callouts. Three appendices do the heavy lifting the chapters send out to them: Appendix C — The centre of gravity, chapter by chapter collects the ten verdicts in one table, Appendix D — How the numbers were made sets out how the most quotable numbers were actually constructed, and Appendix E — The disputes in full gives the live scholarly disputes in full.
