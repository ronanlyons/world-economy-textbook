1  Deep structures: a world without a centre

Preliminary draft — under review. Published for review; content, figures and citations may still change.

Before prices, the only measure of integration is the diffusion of goods and ideas.

“Ships from Meluhha, ships from Magan, and ships from Dilmun he made tie up alongside the quay of Agade.” — inscription of Sargon of Akkad, c.2300 BCE

Follow one thing

Follow a bead. Sometime around 2500 BCE an artisan in one of the Indus cities took a piece of hard orange carnelian, drilled it lengthwise with a slow stone drill, and etched a white pattern into its surface by a technique that was, for the moment, a Harappan monopoly. The bead travelled. It went down the Indus, along the coast of the Arabian Sea, up the Persian Gulf through the entrepots of Magan and Dilmun, and ended in a grave at Ur, some two thousand kilometres from the hand that made it, and a good deal further by the route it actually took. No contract survives to tell us who carried it, what was paid, or at what price.

The bead itself is the evidence — that two distant economies had touched, that goods and standards moved between them, and that an exchange existed at all. This is how the whole of this chapter must be read: in a world before coins and price-lists, integration is visible only in things, in the diffusion of goods and shared measures rather than in any record of a market.

And the bead carried one more clue, in the direction it travelled. Indus goods reached Sumer, and Meluhhan ships docked in Sumerian ports, while much less Mesopotamian material has been found in the Indus. In a period with no prices to compare, an asymmetry like that is the crudest available sign of who held the initiative. It is also a treacherous one, and this chapter takes it apart later — beginning with the observation that a carnelian bead is exactly the kind of thing that survives to be counted. But it does place the Indus, in its first appearance in this book, among the most active traders of a world that did not yet have a centre.1

Where we are on the arc

This is the first chapter, and it is the one place in the book where the central question cannot yet be asked. The book exists to test a claim — that the weight of the world economy sat in Asia for far longer than we tend to assume — period by period, against the evidence. But “where did the weight sit?” only means something once there is a single connected economy whose weight can be located, and across these three thousand years there was not one. There were several loosely linked riverine cores, trading at the edges, rising and falling on their own clocks, but no integrated system with a centre of gravity to find. So this chapter returns no verdict on the spine; it builds the ground the spine will later stand on, laying down the geography — the monsoon and the three great channels — that will eventually make the Indian Ocean the natural integrator.2

The stage and the cast

The stage was built by geology and weather. A continental collision some fifty million years ago raised the Himalayas, and the mountains organised the climate of half the world: they created the monsoon, the great reversing wind that blows from the south-west in the northern summer and swings back from the north-east in winter, and they fed the river basins — the Indus, the Ganges, and by analogy the Yellow and Yangtze — where the first surplus-producing societies grew up.

The monsoon’s deepest gift was reliability. Because the wind reversed on a known schedule, a sailor could ride out on one season’s breath and return on the other, which made predictable round-trip voyaging possible across the Indian Ocean in a way the Atlantic and Pacific could not match. That reliability is what the geographer Michael Pearson called the ocean’s “deep structure,” and it is the master variable of this whole book.

But it set possibilities, not outcomes — a stage, not a script. Three corridors were born in these millennia and would carry goods, people and ideas for the next four thousand years: the maritime Indian Ocean, the desert-and-oasis route later called the Silk Road, and the great Eurasian steppe. Around them sat the cast — the riverine cores that could generate a tradable surplus.3

TipDramatis personae

The economic actors of c.3500–500 BCE, profiled east to west. There is no centre yet, so these are loosely linked cores rather than partners in one system. India appears in every chapter at the fullest depth — here as the Indus civilisation, the period’s most active long-distance trader on the evidence that survives — a qualification the chapter goes on to make good. The steppe comes last not as a periphery but as the era’s dynamic engine.

TipThe forming East Asian core

The Yellow and Yangtze valleys were taking shape as an agrarian world in their own right while the Gulf trade ran in the west, but they sat largely outside it. Wild rice was being gathered along the Yangtze from about 8000 BCE, cultivated from about 6000, and fully domesticated in the lower Yangtze only around 4000 to 3000 BCE, and by 3000 BCE farming had spread across much of what would become the East Asian core; the first Chinese state emerged around 1800 BCE. This was a populous, river-fed agrarian base of the same broad type as Mesopotamia or the Indus, generating surplus and supporting dense settlement, but it grew up on its own metabolism rather than as a node in the western relay.4

What marks the core out for this period is how late the western bronze-and-chariot package reached it. The earliest known chariots come from Sintashta graves in the Urals, before 2000 BCE; the type was in the Near East by the nineteenth century BCE, yet it did not appear in China until about 1200 BCE — arriving by way of the steppe, not the Gulf. The same was broadly true of the metallurgy that defined the Bronze Age elsewhere. So the East Asian core was forming on a parallel track, connected to the wider system, if at all, only through the long grass corridor to its north and west.5

Two states then carried the core through the rest of this chapter’s span, and the first of them left records.

The Shang (c.1600–1046 BCE) were northern China’s first securely documented state, and they cast bronze at something close to industrial scale: foundries at Zhengzhou and Anyang, ritual vessels surviving in the thousands, the largest of them weighing over 800 kilograms. Their expansion followed commodities, north-east toward the Bohai salt and south toward metal, with jade, ivory, cowries and turquoise moving on top of that. They also left the oracle bones, the earliest Chinese writing, and the contrast with Mesopotamia is sharp enough to be worth pausing on: cuneiform began as accounting, while the oracle bones are records of divination. The two earliest writing systems in this book were made to preserve different things, which shapes what each civilisation can now be asked about. One thing the Shang did not invent was the chariot, in royal use by about 1200 BCE and unmistakably a debt to the steppe. The core was independent, but it was not sealed.6

The Zhou who followed show state capacity arriving well before anything an economist would call a market.

Western Zhou (1045–771 BCE) was a patrimonial arrangement in von Glahn’s reading: the king shared authority with noble houses and lived off rights over land and labour, a domain state rather than a tax state, its three ministries named for Lands, Construction and Horses. Exchange ran between aristocratic lineages, and markets in any ordinary sense were absent; this was a world of obligation rather than of wages. Eastern Zhou (770–256 BCE), and the Warring States contest that ran on to Qin’s unification in 221, then ground capacity out of endemic war — conscription, censuses, uniform weights, deliberate grain-price smoothing by about 400 BCE, primogeniture abolished in the 350s — while iron lifted farm output and coinage pulled interregional trade and specialisation, iron in Hann, salt in Qi, gold in Chu. The sequence is the counter-intuitive part. The instinct is to assume markets came first and states arrived later to regulate them; here the state came first, by a wide margin.7

A warning has to travel with all of this, and it is sharper than a general complaint about thin evidence. The bias is in the writing medium. Mesopotamia kept its accounts on clay, which survives when a city burns; early China kept its on bamboo and wood slips, which do not begin to survive in the archaeological record until about the fifth century BCE. What we have from Shang China is therefore inscriptions meant for display, above all the oracle bones, and Wang Haicheng’s point is that the vast majority of bones cracked for divination were never inscribed at all, while everyday administrative documents were almost certainly written on perishable materials and are simply gone. The graph for a bundle of writing slips appears in the divination texts themselves, so the Shang were keeping records on slips; we just cannot read them.

So a comparison between a bureaucratic, account-keeping Mesopotamia and a ritual, divining China may be substantially a comparison between clay and bamboo. That does not make the western record false, but it does mean the eastern core is under-documented for this era rather than demonstrably marginal, and that absence of data is not absence of activity.8

TipThe Indus civilisation — the agency-holder

The Indus, or Harappan, civilisation grew up along the Indus and its tributaries from roughly 3000 BCE, when its first cities appeared at much the same moment as the Early Dynastic cities of Sumer, and it reached its mature urban phase between about 2600 and 1900 BCE. Its base was agrarian, like every economy in this chapter. What set the Harappans apart was how much of what they built on it had been designed: Mohenjo-daro and Harappa were laid out to a plan, with gridded streets, fired-brick housing and elaborate covered drains, at a degree of urban engineering with no contemporary equal. Mohenjo-daro covered more than 200 hectares, within reach of Uruk’s 250, and Harappan urban sites ran from about 50 hectares to over 200. The distinguishing feature was not size but deliberation.

The settlement pattern is the stranger fact. Mature Harappan material turns up at more than a thousand sites spread over more than a million square kilometres, and of those only five count as urban: Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Ganweriwala, Rakhigarhi and Dholavira. The rest are typically around five hectares.

Those five stand far apart, 280 kilometres from Mohenjo-daro to Ganweriwala and 600 from Harappa to Mohenjo-daro, across a territory the size of western Europe. Mesopotamia in the same centuries was a crowded landscape of neighbouring cities within a few days’ walk of one another. The Indus was a handful of very large places in a very large space, and whatever held it together was not proximity.

Two of the things this economy stood on were domesticated locally, which matters for a region routinely written up as a recipient of other people’s innovations: zebu cattle at or around Mehrgarh, and the earliest evidence anywhere for Indian tree cotton, on which a textile tradition rests that later chapters follow for four thousand years.

Mehrgarh’s own dating has just moved, and sharply: the excavation’s surviving team, radiocarbon-dating tooth enamel rather than the root-contaminated charcoal the old chronology rested on, now put its aceramic Neolithic at 5250 to 4650 BCE rather than beginning around 7000 or 8000. The cotton, from a grave in that phase, moves with it. Nothing about the local-domestication argument depends on the older dates, but a great deal of popular writing about Mehrgarh does.10

Two features of the internal economy stand out, and the second is genuinely puzzling. The first was standardisation, and it went further than the phrase usually implies. Harappan cubical stone weights double in a binary sequence up to a standard of about 13.7 grams and then continue in decimal multiples, the largest at Mohenjo-daro reaching almost eleven kilograms — a system, not a convention, and one exported as far as the Gulf. Fired bricks hold a 1:2:4 ratio across the whole settled zone. That last uniformity is where the state instinct should be resisted: Kenoyer’s explanation is not a building code but a class of brickmakers whose craft tradition spread from settlement to settlement. Alongside the weights ran a corpus of inscribed seals, and the two together are the apparatus of an economy in which goods were measured, marked and exchanged to common conventions over long distances.

The second feature is what the cities lack, and the usual shorthand overstates it. There are no royal tombs, no monumental religious architecture, no royal iconography and no depictions of warfare. Two entries on that list need care: temples are not securely absent, since Kenoyer allows that House AI at Mohenjo-daro may have been one; and no depictions of warfare is not the same as no warfare, because skeletal remains from Harappa carry clear evidence of interpersonal violence. What is absent is a palace quarter — the largest buildings are scattered across the mounds rather than gathered into a royal precinct — and the structures long called granaries have gone too, having produced no charred grain, no storage vessels and no sealings. What these cities were reticent about was the display of personal power, which is not the same as being socially flat: Possehl, who denies this was a state, still writes of upper ranks and a social pyramid, and remarks that the Great Bath was not the playground of the Indus hoi polloi.11

How such a civilisation was governed, and how its surplus was gathered without the visible apparatus of monarchy, is the liveliest question in Indus archaeology, and it is worth giving students the argument rather than a verdict. Three positions are in play.

They run as a spectrum rather than a quarrel. Kenoyer argues for rulers of a kind, reading the weight system as established by them, plausibly for taxation. Possehl denies a state in the archaic sense — no king, no royal apparatus — but affirms elites, ranks and authority perhaps sitting in councils. Adam Green goes further, arguing for collective governance in which many intersecting institutions rather than any ruling class coordinated labour and produced the public goods. Appendix E sets the three out, and the evidence that divides them: it turns largely on where the seals were found, and the same distribution supports Green’s reading and Possehl’s depending how it is weighed.

One fact cuts across all three readings: no Indus seal has ever been found in a burial, which is exactly where Egypt and Mesopotamia put the marks of individual status.

What the three agree on is enough to carry the economic argument without settling the dispute. There were no kings, no royal display and no depictions of warfare; there was extensive investment in urban public goods — house-by-house wells, bathing platforms, covered drains — and there was a standardisation apparatus reaching from villages to the Gulf. A society can build all of that without anyone’s name on it.

This matters beyond the Indus. The chapters that follow spend a great deal of time on states that taxed, borrowed, minted and spent, and it is easy to slide from that into assuming an economy needs a visible sovereign before it can build anything. The Harappans built more urban infrastructure per head than any of their contemporaries and left no king’s name attached to any of it. Who commanded that effort is genuinely unresolved.12

With no prices and no ledgers, the integration the Harappans achieved cannot be read off a market. It is read from the things themselves: Harappan seals, cubical weights and above all etched carnelian beads turn up in Mesopotamian contexts, and carnelian from the Indus borderlands was moving north through the Caucasus by the late fourth millennium. Indus craft and Indus measures in distant markets, not any record of transactions, are the evidence that a long-distance economy existed at all.13

Externally, the Harappans ran the period’s great long-distance trade, the Gulf route that linked the Indus — the Meluhha of the cuneiform records — to Mesopotamia through the intermediary ports of Magan, on the Oman coast, and Dilmun, the entrepot on Bahrain.

What moved is set out in the trade profile below; two qualifications matter more than the inventory. The first is that few ships sailed the whole way. The traffic moved in stages, and in the Ur III period Mesopotamian boats went only as far as Magan, and later only to Dilmun, even though most of what they collected there had started in India. Dilmun grew rich precisely because the route was broken in the middle. The second is that the westward flow was not the whole story. Meluhhan boats put in at Sargon’s quay at Akkad, and an Akkadian seal inscription names an interpreter of Meluhha — there were Harappans in Mesopotamia, and enough of them to need translating. But Mesopotamian weights have turned up at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, and in one Harappan cemetery a young woman was buried wrapped in a reed shroud inside a wooden coffin, a rite unknown in the Indus world and familiar from Ur and Kish. Somebody from Mesopotamia lived, and died, in India.14

What survives is not a clean asymmetry but a changing one. Through the mature phase the initiative ran west: Harappan goods, standards and people are far more visible in Mesopotamia than the reverse, and no Mesopotamian commercial text records trade with Meluhha at all, the references being royal and tributary. Later the balance shifted, once Harappan ports on the Makran coast came within reach of sailors who did not have to cross open-ocean monsoon water, and the carrying passed increasingly to Mesopotamians and Dilmunites. Agency followed the sailing conditions and the position of the ports rather than belonging to anyone. Carry that forward carefully: who carried the goods is a fact about shipping capability and who bore the voyage risk, not about who captured the gains, which turns on each side’s alternatives — and Mesopotamia had the deeper pockets. The proposition the rest of this book uses is the safer one, that whoever could reach the other party on their own terms was better placed to take the larger share.

One further caution bites hardest of all, and it is about what each side sent. Indus exports were carnelian beads, seals, ivory and stone weights: hard, distinctive, durable objects that survive four thousand years and are identifiable at a glance as Harappan. Mesopotamia sent back silver, tin, wool, oil and grain, which were consumed, remelted, rotted, or survive without being identifiable as Mesopotamian at all. An exchange of exactly equal value in both directions would leave a lopsided record, tilted the way this one is tilted. That does not dissolve the asymmetry — the Harappan presence includes people and standards, not only goods — but it does mean the record cannot carry the weight the strong version of the claim puts on it. It is the same survival logic the chapter uses in the Indus’s favour elsewhere, running the other way.1516

The urban phase ended around 1900 BCE: the great cities were given up, the weights-and-seals apparatus fell out of use, and the population shifted east toward the Ganges. The decline was broadly coincident with the weakening of the monsoon at the 4.2 ka event, and the temptation to read one as the cause of the other should be resisted — starting with the arithmetic. The 4.2 ka event is around 2200 BCE and the de-urbanisation around 1900: three centuries apart, which is a long time for a cause to take effect. The palaeoclimate record shows no drought of the right timing and depth, and both standard authorities decline the climate explanation anyway, Possehl for an internal ideological failure and Kenoyer for flooding routed through a crisis of legitimacy. Both attribute the drying of the Ghaggar-Hakra to tectonic change rather than monsoon failure, which detaches the most-cited piece of hydrological evidence from the climate story altogether. Appendix E sets the four readings against each other. On all of them, “de-urbanisation” fits better than “collapse”.17

TipDilmun — the Gulf entrepot

Dilmun, centred on Bahrain, was the junction of the Gulf trade that linked the Indus world to Mesopotamia, at its height between about 2600 and 1700 BCE. The early third-millennium Dilmun of the cuneiform texts most likely lay in eastern Arabia, around Tarut; Bahrain itself shows little substantial settlement before about 2200 BCE. Goods moved up the Gulf in stages: the Indus (known to the Sumerians as Meluhha) supplied carnelian, ivory, hardwoods, lapis and etched beads; Magan, on the Oman side, supplied copper; and Dilmun sat in the middle, gathering these and passing them on into Sumer. Its position made it the natural meeting point between the seaborne traffic of the lower Gulf and the river cities at its head.19

The economic logic is the one the module returns to again and again. Dilmun produced little of what it traded. Its wealth came from controlling a chokepoint — re-exporting Indus and Magan goods into the Mesopotamian demand-core and taking a cut on the way through. Value was captured not by making the carnelian or smelting the copper but by sitting astride the route along which they had to pass. That is the bottleneck-and-toll pattern: whoever controls the narrow passage between a supplier and a hungry market can levy the difference, and the entrepot grows rich on other people’s goods.20

Two things about Dilmun cut against what an entrepôt is supposed to look like. The first is that it was not culturally derivative. Harriet Crawford’s summary is that Dilmun’s material culture owed “remarkably little either to its immediate neighbours, or to the trading partners at either end of the trade routes”, and the seals make the point concretely: Dilmun used round stamp seals with its own iconography, Mesopotamia used cylinder seals, the Indus used square stamps. Three partners in one trading system, three different ways of sealing a transaction. A place that lived by intermediation did not become a blend of the people it intermediated between.

The second is stranger and bears directly on an argument this chapter has been building. Dilmun was in sustained contact with three literate civilisations — Mesopotamia, Susa and the Indus — and ran exactly the kind of complex, repeated, long-distance transactions that writing is supposed to have emerged to keep track of. Yet we do not know that it had a writing system, and no Dilmun script has been identified. Crawford, invoking the same argument about the origins of cuneiform used earlier in this chapter, finds it hard to believe that such canny operators managed without one, and allows that the need may have been met in other ways. Either way the case is a useful check. If writing came out of the need to track transactions, here is a society with the transactions and, so far as anyone can show, without the writing.21

Dilmun’s position also shaped who did the sailing. Few vessels ran the whole route: Mesopotamian boats went as far as Magan, and later only as far as Dilmun, while the Harappans worked the eastern leg, so the middle of the chain was where the goods changed hands and the margin was taken. That is why the entrepot mattered more than its own production ever warranted. Reading the direction and the range of the ships, in the absence of prices, is one of the few ways this period allows of inferring who was active and who was being supplied.22

TipMesopotamia and Uruk — the demand core

Mesopotamia was the period’s most commercially central node, and Uruk was its showpiece.

The scale of what happened there is easier to grasp as a rate than as a size. Through the proto-urban centuries, settlements in the region seldom passed 20 hectares, and counters and stamp seals were enough to keep track of what they produced. Then, by the end of the Late Uruk period, Uruk expanded to at least 250 hectares, some 2.5 square kilometres, holding perhaps 40,000 to 50,000 people. Nissen puts the city at that size around 3300 BCE, at the close of the sequence rather than its opening. That is more than a twelvefold increase in built area, and the world had not seen anything like it. It rested on an organised hinterland, whose size is routinely overstated: the radii in circulation are limits on early states in general, or measurements of other cities, rather than of Uruk, and the tax share usually quoted is an inference from agrarian history at large rather than a figure from any Sumerian record. What can be said is that water changed the arithmetic. River transport extended the catchment for bulk goods enormously, which is why the alluvium’s cities sat on watercourses.

Two cautions. These are reconstructions, and the specialists are more reticent than the synthesis — Emberling opens his own chapter with cities that “may have housed 50,000 people or more” and then declines to say what population makes a settlement a city at all, which is the harder question. And the southern alluvium was not the only place cities were forming: Tell Brak, 750 kilometres north, had reached about 130 hectares before 3500 BCE, so any account making southern irrigation the cause of Mesopotamian urbanism has a northern problem.24

Growth on that scale had to be administered, and the administrative technology is what survives. Beveled-rim bowls, mould-made in the millions and mostly holding the same amount, imply a standard unit of measure and, on the usual reading, the issue of rations; the cylinder seal answered the same problem of keeping track of a large and stratified population. Writing came out of that effort. The earliest tablets did not represent spoken language at all and were, in Hans Nissen’s phrase, barely more than an extension of the techniques of accounting. That writing was invented to do bookkeeping and only later learned to carry literature is one of the more useful things this period has to say about how economies build the tools they need.25

The clearest window onto how that economy worked was textile manufacture. Uruk’s state workshops are credited with as many as 9,000 women — on the order of a fifth of the whole city — spinning and weaving wool into cloth that the temple and palace then disbursed or traded.

That figure needs handling with care. It comes from Scott, who writes a few paragraphs later that reading such evidence back from the Ur III archives to the Uruk period “is highly questionable”, and the archaic Uruk tablets are largely undeciphered administrative accounts that will not support a workforce headcount of that precision. An establishment of that size, fed and housed from central stores and producing on central account, looks like a command economy: goods flowed up to the institution as grain and wool, and back down as rations and finished cloth, in the redistributive pattern Karl Polanyi placed at the heart of the early Near East. Whether that was the whole story is the live debate. The same society also ran private loans, partnerships and price-sensitive long-distance ventures, which point to genuine market exchange alongside the palace ledger. Redistribution and market dealing coexisted, and the balance between them remains contested rather than settled.

Two cautions belong with that, and the first is chronological. The archives documenting administered redistribution most fully are not Uruk’s but those of the Third Dynasty of Ur, around 2100 BCE, more than a millennium later; Emberling treats the great collection-and-redistribution centres as an innovation of that empire rather than a feature of the first cities. Reading Ur III practice back onto Uruk is a common move and not a safe one. The second is that the strongest version of the redistributive case has already been walked back, on the evidence of the very archive it was built from. Appendix E has both.26

Money and credit were already mature, well before any coin was struck. Silver and barley served as units of account and means of payment, so prices existed even though coinage did not. Interest-bearing debt is securely attested from the later third millennium and may reach back to around 3000 BCE; the rates were later codified under Hammurabi at 20 per cent on silver and 33 1/3 per cent on barley. Debt could spiral into bondage, and rulers periodically wiped the slate: in about 2400 BCE Enmetena of Lagash declared amargi, “freedom”, cancelling debts — the first recorded use of the word in a political document. Credit belonged to this economy from the start. What arrived later was minted coinage, and the continuous price series that came with it.27

What made Mesopotamia the demand pole was its geology. The southern alluvium was rich in silt and grain but had almost no metal, building stone or timber of its own, so the materials of an urban civilisation had to be imported. Even Uruk’s monumental architecture leaned on stone hauled some eighty kilometres, and the metals, hard stones and fine woods came from much further. An integrated trading sphere — the “Uruk world system” — already linked the Iranian plateau to the eastern Mediterranean between about 3500 and 3100 BCE, then contracted around 3000 BCE as the long-distance networks thinned and an era of warring city-states began. The luxuries it had carried travelled astonishing distances: lapis lazuli from Badakhshan in Afghanistan moved roughly 2,500 kilometres to reach Mesopotamia, and onward to Egypt. The resource-poor core, in other words, generated the demand that pulled goods across half a continent.28

The mechanics of that import trade are best seen in the early second-millennium tin caravans between Assur and the Anatolian colony at Kanesh. Donkey trains of fifty or more animals carried tin and textiles roughly 950 kilometres on a journey of about fifty days, exchanging them for silver.

The price gap was the point. Quoted in silver, which served as the period’s unit of account, 100 shekels of tin cost about 6.7 shekels of silver at Assur, about 10 at Mari, and about 14.3 in Anatolia, where tin was scarcer and dearer; the textiles fetched three to four times their Assyrian price. Merchants reckoned gross mark-ups near 100 per cent on the metal and 200 per cent on the cloth. Those are mark-ups and not profits: the fifty-day journey, the fodder, the drivers, the tolls, the agent’s commission and something like a hundred days of tied-up capital all came out of them first. Nor is this arbitrage, which is riskless and simultaneous; it is a capital-committing venture across fifty days and 950 kilometres, and the returns had to compensate the risk or nobody would have gone.

That price gap is the most useful number the period yields. Tin cost roughly twice as much in Anatolia as at Assur, and the gap persisted for generations while tonnages moved along the route.

A gap that stable is not a market failing to integrate. It is the cost of integration, quoted: what it took to put a shekel of tin 950 kilometres further west, in donkeys, drivers, fodder, tolls, protection and time. Read that way, Assur and Kanesh were about as integrated as the technology of the day allowed, and the measure of later integration is not that such gaps disappear but that they shrink toward the physical cost of carriage. That is the same test the rest of this book applies to grain in the nineteenth century and to containers in the twentieth, run on the only evidence the Bronze Age left. To the south-east, a separate maritime arm ran down the Gulf: Sumer drew copper from Magan (Oman) and exotic goods from Meluhha (the Indus), traded through the entrepot of Dilmun (Bahrain), with one Ur merchant, Ea-nasir, assembling fifty-one investors for a single Dilmun venture. Tin, copper, lapis and carnelian flowed in; the demand core sat at the western end of the longest exchange networks of the age.29

TipEgypt — the Nile apart

Egypt was a riverine core of the first rank, but it stood largely outside the Gulf system that linked the Indus to Mesopotamia. Its wealth came from the Nile: a narrow ribbon of irrigated land whose predictable flood underwrote dense agriculture and a centralised state from unification around 3100 BCE, and through the Old, Middle and New Kingdoms from about 2686 — with the two Intermediate Periods as genuine interruptions to that centralisation rather than footnotes to it. Early Dynastic Egypt matters here for a further reason: it is the state contemporary with Uruk, and the period of the documented Egyptian contacts with the Uruk world. Where Mesopotamia depended on uncertain rainfall and rivers, Egypt’s annual inundation gave it an agrarian base reliable enough to sustain monumental building and a deep bureaucracy, a precocious complexity to rival anything in the Near East.31

Egypt traded, but along its own axes rather than through the Gulf. It looked south to Punt for incense, ivory and exotic goods, and east through the Sinai and the Red Sea margins for copper, turquoise and contact with the Levant. Lapis lazuli from Afghanistan reached Egypt by overland relay, a reminder that it was tied into the wider Bronze Age web at the edges. But its trading face pointed down the Nile corridor and across the Sinai land bridge, not down the Persian Gulf, and the Indus-to-Sumer relay ran without it.32

Where Egypt put its surplus is the more interesting question, and the answer is not “into cities”. John Baines argues that the values of early Egyptian rulers and elites seem not to have focused on cities at all. Power was presented across a landscape rather than concentrated in an urban form: dispersed mortuary monuments and necropolis sites, movement between them, and a careful interplay of display and concealment. Egyptian cities were distinctive in layout, constrained by the floodplain and overshadowed by those monuments, and it was only later, by the New Kingdom, that the society became more city-focused and its cities came to resemble those elsewhere in the Near East.

Set that beside the other two riverine cores and this chapter has a comparison worth making. Three societies of roughly the same era each generated a large agricultural surplus, and each put it somewhere different. Uruk put it into the city itself and into the apparatus for running one: walls, workshops, ration bowls turned out in the millions, and the accounting that became writing. The Harappans put it into infrastructure that nobody signed, with the seals that recorded claims on resources sitting in ordinary households rather than in palaces. Egypt put it into monuments to named individuals, spread along a river. None of these is a market economy, and the differences between them are not differences of wealth. They are differences in who could command the surplus and what they wanted it for, which is a question this book asks in every later chapter with better data and rarely a clearer contrast.33

There is a further reason to keep Egypt in this chapter rather than filing it as a magnificent irrelevance, and it comes from the economics of its record-keeping. Brian Muhs argues that the Egyptian state expanded the use of writing over three millennia for a straightforward fiscal reason: to document and exact taxes more efficiently, and so to run redistribution better. But the writing did something its promoters did not intend. Once titles and transfers could be documented and enforced, the cost of making a private transaction fell relative to the cost of going through the institution, and private exchange grew at redistribution’s expense. Silver, coming into use as a common measure of value and store of wealth, lowered those costs again for high-value dealings. The state then began taxing transfers in silver, which gave it a direct fiscal interest in documenting and enforcing private property rights still further.

That loop runs through the rest of this book. An administrative technology adopted to make extraction work better ended up lowering the transaction costs of the private exchange that competed with extraction, and the state’s own revenue interest then pushed it into underwriting the competition. It is the same insight economists reach for when they ask why some activity happens inside an organisation and some through a market, and Egypt supplies a three-thousand-year run of it. Set beside Uruk, where writing began as accounting and stayed institutional for a very long time, and beside the Phoenician alphabet later in this chapter, which put writing in a merchant’s hands, the point is that the same technology can push an economy in opposite directions depending on who can afford it and what the state wants from it.34

So Egypt is best read as precocious but separate: an early, rich, highly organised state that developed in parallel with the Gulf system rather than as part of it. It mattered enormously in the later eastern-Mediterranean world — by the fourteenth century BCE its correspondence with Hatti, Babylon and the Aegean shows it deep in the palace-economy network whose collapse around 1177 BCE it barely survived. For the first integration of the Gulf, though, it is a neighbouring core, not a participant.35

TipThe steppe — the dynamic engine

The Eurasian steppe was no periphery but the era’s main artery: an unbroken grass corridor running some 7,000 km from eastern Europe to the Great Wall of China. What turned this ribbon of grass into a power-and-information highway was the horse. A herder on foot could manage about 200 sheep; mounted, the same herder could manage some 500, ranging 50 to 60 kilometres in a working day around them. Read that figure carefully: it is a herdsman’s daily range, not the pace of a migration. Households moving with their animals covered something closer to 10 to 20 kilometres on a day they moved, and nearer nine or ten averaged across a whole migration, held to the speed of the herd and to the grass it had to stop and eat. What mounted herding bought was reach, and reach is what made the corridor the fastest channel for moving people, animals and news across the continent.37

A cluster of innovations compounded the advantage. The wheel, and then the chariot — the earliest known examples of which come from Sintashta graves in the Urals, before 2000 BCE — spread outward along the grass, reaching the Near East by the nineteenth century BCE and China only around 1200 BCE. Alongside these ran what Andrew Sherratt called a “secondary-products revolution”: the use of animals for traction, wool and dairy rather than meat alone. That thesis is contested — whether it was a single fourth-millennium event or a gradual, patchy adoption is debated — but the underlying shift, animals exploited while alive, was real and it multiplied what a pastoral economy could yield.38

The corridor was also a vector of migration. The Yamnaya expansion out of the Pontic-Caspian steppe around 3000 BCE has been associated, on present ancient-DNA readings, with a large influx of steppe-derived ancestry into Europe — as much as 90 per cent of the gene pool in Britain at the Bell Beaker transition around 2450 BCE, and about 75 per cent in Corded Ware burials in Germany, rather than a single figure for the continent — a striking figure that should be held cautiously, since the genetics and its demographic interpretation are still moving. The steppe, then, was a channel and an engine at once: it raided the settled cores, traded with them, and at times poured into them, shaping the riverine worlds it bordered rather than merely lying beyond them.39

The period on its own terms

Before the cycles, a word about what the base cost. Every economy in this chapter stood on farming, and farming was not obviously a bargain for the people who did it.

The skeletal record across the agricultural transition, read at sites worldwide, mostly shows the same direction of travel: shorter stature, more dental caries as fermentable carbohydrates displaced a mixed forager diet, more enamel defects from childhood stress, and heavier loads of infectious disease as people settled at density alongside their animals and their refuse. Sedentism brought contaminated water, vermin, poor sanitation, harvest failure and soil exhaustion, and it narrowed the variety of what was eaten. Social inequality appears alongside all of this. The finding is not universal, and bioarchaeologists are careful about it, since any one skeletal marker can have several causes and a minority of studies run the other way. But the broad pattern is the physical counterpart of the Malthusian result set out at the end of this chapter: the gains from better technology and better land went into more people rather than into better-fed ones. Agriculture was a way of supporting numbers, not a way of living well, and that is the yardstick this book carries forward.41

The three cycles are worth naming at the outset, because the shape returns in every later chapter. An early Uruk sphere reached from the Iranian plateau to the Mediterranean and then contracted. Between the second and the third sits a gap of some three centuries that no trading cycle fills, and what carried that gap was a technology rather than a trade: the horse, the wheel and the chariot, remaking on the steppe what movement cost.

Deep time: the stage is built. The board was assembled long before there was anything on it to call an economy. The collision that raised the Himalayas organised the climate of half the world, and the section above has already set out what it produced: the reversing monsoon, the river basins that fed the first surplus-producing societies, and the three corridors that carry goods for the rest of this book. Cycle one: the first integration, and its contraction (c.3500–3000 BCE). The earliest integrated trading sphere the record shows did not run through the Gulf at all, but overland across the northern Near East. From roughly 3500 to 3100 BCE a “Uruk world system” linked the Iranian plateau to the eastern Mediterranean, drawing distant materials toward the Mesopotamian alluvium where the world’s first true cities were rising. Uruk itself was already a demand-core of startling size: by around 3300 BCE it covered some 250 hectares and held perhaps forty to fifty thousand people. That is a settled area rather than an intramural one: Uruk’s wall belongs to the Early Dynastic period, four centuries later. Its state textile workshops are credited with as many as nine thousand women, on the order of a fifth of the city, subject to the caution entered above.

Such a place pulled goods from far away.

Lapis lazuli from the mines of Badakhshan in north-eastern Afghanistan travelled roughly 2,500 kilometres to reach Mesopotamia, and the same blue stone reached Egypt at roughly the same period. This was integration in its archaic form, measurable not by prices but by the diffusion of materials far from their sources.

Then it came apart. Around 3000 BCE the Uruk system contracted: the long-distance exchange network thinned and an era of warring city-states opened in its place. Lapis itself kept coming — the greatest concentration of it outside Afghanistan is the Royal Cemetery of Ur, five centuries after the supposed break — so what changed was the scale and the politics of the traffic rather than its existence. The period’s first paired peak-and-fall therefore happened before the Gulf trade that the older accounts foreground, which means the integration-then-disintegration rhythm that defines this whole story began one cycle earlier than is often assumed.43

Cycle two ends: the Harappan cities empty (c.1900 BCE). The mature urban phase gave way to de-urbanisation and an eastward shift of population, and the great planned cities lost the density that had defined them. Between the cycles: the steppe remade what movement cost. The Harappan cities emptied around 1900 BCE, and the eastern Mediterranean system that follows did not come together until the sixteenth century. What fills it is usually said to be a technology rather than a trading cycle, and the technology is real enough — it came from the great grass corridor to the north of every core in this chapter.

But the claim that no trading system occupies the interval will not survive a look at this chapter’s own evidence.

The Assyrian tin trade between Assur and Kanesh ran from about 1950 to 1750 BCE, squarely inside it, and it is the best-documented commercial network anywhere in these three thousand years: the price ratios, the caravan logistics, the credit and the merchant families all come from there. The Mari archives and the Old Babylonian Gulf trade sit in the same window. What the interval lacks is not commerce but a maritime cycle of the kind that brackets it — and the Assyrian material is vivid because a merchant quarter’s private papers happened to survive, which is a fact about archives rather than about the economy.

Read the three cycles, then, as three horizons of surviving evidence that coincide with three real expansions, not as an account of when trade did and did not happen.

What the interval does carry, and the bracketing cycles do not, is a change in what movement cost. The steppe profile above sets out the technology; the point to take forward is its pace.

The domestic horse lineage that spread across Eurasia expands only from about 2200 BCE, which makes horse and chariot near-contemporaries; and the earliest known chariots, from Sintashta graves east of the Urals before 2000 BCE, reached the Near East within a century or two and China only around 1200. Nine hundred years for one technology to cross a continent is the diffusion speed of this world, and it is the baseline against which every later chapter’s should be read.47

What riding did to the economy shows up in two things, and only one of them is a speed.

The first is scale. A herder on foot managed perhaps two hundred animals; mounted, he could hold around five hundred and graze the deep interior. That is a productivity gain of roughly 150 per cent, in the ordinary sense of the term.

The second is reach, which is not the same thing as speed, and the difference is where the usual story goes wrong. Daily rates barely moved: a household travelling with its herds made 10 to 20 kilometres on a day it moved at all, a party on foot 18 to 25, a rider travelling light 30 to 45.

The herding figure flatters it further, because the migration was itself the grazing. Kazakh auls covering 800 to 1,000 kilometres took 90 to 110 days over some seventy halts — a net advance of nine or ten kilometres a day. A Lorestan tribe’s ninety-kilometre move was reckoned a ten-day trek. Nine to eleven kilometres a day turns up in region after region.

So moving with animals was slower than walking, and riding roughly doubled a traveller’s pace rather than transforming it.

What changed by an order of magnitude was range. Anthony’s Yamnaya herders moved within an annual orbit of under 50 kilometres, while mounted raiding parties struck hundreds of kilometres out and drove livestock home. The economy’s orbit stayed small. The reach of force and information did not.

That separation is the thing to carry forward, because the two never rejoin. The spectacular courier speeds this period is often credited with — the Persian road, the Roman post, the Mongol relay — belong to later societies that built stations and kept remounts at them. The Venetian letter, the telegraph and the container ship are all later chapters of the same story.

Two distinctions inside the word “nomad” matter here. Ingold’s is between living off a dead animal and living off a live one: milk, blood and wool are a renewable yield where meat is terminal, and the move to secondary products is what turns a herd from a larder into an income.

The second is a warning against reading any of this as environmental destiny. Specialist pastoralism tends to dominate where other farming cannot succeed. But the forest steppes of southern Russia and northern Kazakhstan grow wheat without irrigation today, and were held by herders from the Eneolithic to the twentieth century anyway. Farming was possible there. Herders kept the ground for two thousand years regardless.

Hold that against this chapter’s closing debate on how much weight geography should carry.48

The consequences ran outward from there. Bigger herds needed more land, land meant conflict, conflict meant alliances, and alliances were bought with elite goods — so long-distance exchange was partly a by-product of horsemanship. These tools also powered the Indo-European expansions that reshaped the genetic and linguistic map of Eurasia, though the migration-and-linguistics detail is contested terrain and best treated lightly. The steppe was no periphery in this period; it was the era’s dynamic engine.49

Cycle three: the Late Bronze Age was already an age of globalisation. By the fourteenth century BCE the eastern Mediterranean and the Near East ran as one working system. Egypt, Hatti, Babylonia, Assyria, Mittani, Mycenaean Greece, Cyprus — the Alashiya of the records, a metal-rich island famous for its copper — and the Syrian port of Ugarit were all in sustained contact with each other.

Their post survives.

The Amarna letters, some 350 letters, from a corpus of 382 tablets, written between about 1365 and 1335 BCE, carry correspondence reaching from Nubia to the Aegean to Iran, and they are written in Akkadian, the diplomatic lingua franca of the day, which almost none of the senders spoke at home. A shared script, a shared protocol and a shared honorific vocabulary — kings addressed one another as “my brother” — are together the strongest single indicator of system-hood that the period offers.

Specialisation was visible in what moved: Cypriot copper, Nubian gold, Afghan lapis, Baltic amber in Mycenaean graves. Hittite iron belongs on a different list — rare, prestigious, and known largely from a single royal letter about a dagger blade, it was not a traded commodity in the way the others were.

The system had rules, too. Its conflicts were wars of readjustment rather than annihilation, and its kings married each other’s daughters, complained in writing about late consignments of gold, and went on trading. What that correspondence describes is trade wearing court dress: royal “gifts” carried explicit reciprocity, stated weights and complaints about quality, and the line between gift-exchange and commerce is one worth refusing to draw too sharply. Cline calls the result a golden age of internationalism, and puts the globalised economy that defined it at three hundred years and more.50

Cycle three ends: the Collapse (c.1177 BCE). Then the densely interconnected palace economies of the eastern Mediterranean failed together, and they failed as a system.

Their dependence on long-distance metal shows the exposure plainly: bronze needed tin, tin sat almost nowhere near the cities that used it, so it had to travel, and its role, as Carol Bell put it, was probably not far different from crude oil’s today.

When the web tore around 1177 BCE the cost was systemic.

Mainland Greece may have lost forty to sixty per cent of its people, falling from perhaps six hundred thousand to around three hundred and thirty thousand, and Babylonia possibly as much as three-quarters before any recovery. Ugarit, destroyed around 1190 BCE, saw some eight thousand inhabitants flee and was not reoccupied for roughly six hundred and fifty years.

The collapse was over-determined — earthquake swarm, drought, the movements conventionally called the Sea Peoples, internal rebellion, the cutting of the trade routes — and that is precisely Cline’s argument: once a system is this tightly coupled, no single cause is needed.

The Sea Peoples in particular need handling with care. Cline calls them a convenient scapegoat, a construct assembled by nineteenth-century scholarship, and the tide has turned against reading them as the agent of the collapse rather than one of its symptoms.

This was integration’s first systemic failure, and its lesson, inherited by the whole module, is that a tightly connected system can break in a way a loose one cannot. What followed is the subject of the next section, and it took seven centuries. 52

After the collapse: the Phoenician centuries, c. 1177–500 BCE

The wreckage took a long time to clear, and what replaced the palaces did not look like them. Iron changed the metals arithmetic, because iron ore is nearly everywhere and the tin bottleneck that had made bronze a strategic problem simply dissolved. But iron did not rebuild the trading world. That was done by a handful of small coastal cities on the Levantine shore – Byblos, Sidon, Arwad and above all Tyre – whose Canaanite inhabitants the Greeks called Phoinikes, and by the Cypriots alongside them.

Why those two and not others? The answer that has emerged is not luck.

The Canaanites of the Levantine coast were not newcomers; they were the survivors of the Bronze Age cities, carrying on in the same places under a new name. What they were not was imperial. Carol Bell’s formulation is the sharpest: Cyprus and Phoenicia “were able to seize the day when crisis struck the region”, being “unencumbered by imperial agendas, and already familiar with operating within a decentralised trading environment”, so that their traders “remained open for business, with their primary objective being to generate sufficient returns to continue to trade”. Eric Cline goes further and calls both societies anti-fragile: they did not merely survive the collapse but flourished in the chaos that followed it, the Phoenicians taking the sack of Ugarit and the other port cities as their opportunity to win control of the Mediterranean routes, the Cypriots doing the same with iron goods and iron technology. The economies that failed in 1177 were centralised, redistributive and tightly coupled. What replaced them at the centre of Mediterranean exchange was a set of merchant cities with no empire and no great king – which is the chapter’s lesson about coupling, restated from the other side.53

One document shows the change better than any argument about it. Around 1075 BCE an Egyptian temple official named Wenamun was sent from Karnak to Byblos to buy cedar for a ceremonial barge. That is the shift from the gift economy the Uluburun ship was carrying to a price economy, recorded in one bad-tempered voyage.54

The most famous thing they made was a colour, and it is the cleanest illustration the ancient world offers of a price set by scarce supply meeting status-driven demand rather than by the cost of the raw material.

Purple dye comes from a gland that has to be cut from a living sea snail, with the shell broken to reach it, which is why the archaeological signature of a dye-works is a bed of crushed shells rather than whole ones. The quantities are brutal.

Experimental archaeology puts about two hundred snails into a square of wool 125 centimetres on a side to reach a deep purple, and at least five thousand into a whole cloak; older estimates ran to twenty thousand animals for a garment’s trim alone. A batch took four days at minimum, and the work stank badly enough that Strabo, centuries later, thought it made Tyre unpleasant to live in. It would be wrong to conclude that the labour made the value — if effort made things valuable, every laborious process would be lucrative.

What made purple dear was that an inelastic, labour-bound supply met courts that would pay almost anything for a colour only they were permitted to wear.

States knew exactly where that left them. In a Hittite letter of the early thirteenth century a vassal complains that a raid has cost him his purple-dyers – three groups of them, one his own, one the king’s, one belonging to a god – and the diplomatic effort to recover them is the substance of the letter. Skilled dyers were capital, and were treated as such.55

What drove them west is the interesting question, and it is genuinely open. The traditional answer is metals. Silver was becoming a proto-currency across the Iron Age Near East – thirty-four silver hoards have been recovered from Cisjordan alone – and demand for it was sharpened by three things at once: the collapse of the Bronze Age exchange networks, the Levant’s own lack of silver, and the rise of the Neo-Assyrian empire with its appetite for tribute. Two other things travelled with the pots. One was a script. Short Phoenician inscriptions, mostly personal names and ownership marks, turn up scratched on ordinary locally made jars in the harbour houses of Castillo de Dona Blanca near Gadir – thirty-eight of them from the eighth and seventh centuries – and by the seventh century the Iberians around Gadir were writing too, on objects of trade. This is writing as a merchant’s tool rather than a bureaucracy’s, and it is the counter-case to Uruk. Cuneiform was expensive. It took hundreds of signs, a scribal career to learn them, and it grew out of running a large institution’s accounts. The Phoenician script was a consonantal alphabet of twenty-two letters, one for each consonantal phoneme in the language, and a trader could carry it in his head.

Its history matters because of when it spread. A consonantal alphabet had been adapted to the cuneiform technique at Ugarit in the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BCE, but the schematised, reduced form that became the Phoenician script came into common use around the twelfth century or a little later – in the specialists’ own phrasing, with the reopening of trade links after the collapse. From there it went everywhere: to the Israelites, Moabites, Ammonites, Edomites and Aramaeans, whose national scripts are all essentially its children; westward with the settlements; and into the Aegean, where the Greeks adapted it, adding vowels. The oldest Greek alphabetic inscriptions date to about the second quarter of the eighth century BCE, and the transmission is dated by comparing letter shapes, since the Phoenician forms of the ninth and eighth centuries are the ones the archaic Greek letters most resemble.

The economic point is the one to carry forward. When the cost of learning to record a transaction falls from a career to an afternoon, record-keeping stops being a function of the state. Uruk’s accounting produced a scribal class; Tyre’s produced a merchant class that could write. Both are answers to the same problem, and the second is very much cheaper.59

Reading the period: the four questions

The three thousand years from about 3500 to 500 BCE can be put through the same four questions that drive the rest of this book, with the warning that the answers here are softer than any later chapter’s, because the evidence is thinner and the system looser. They are worth running all the same, because this is where the questions themselves are forged.

The first question was about direction: was the system integrating or disintegrating? The answer was that it did both, repeatedly, and that no single arc ran through the period. An early integrated sphere, the so-called Uruk world system, reached from the Iranian plateau to the eastern Mediterranean around 3500 BCE and then contracted near 3000 BCE as its long-distance networks thinned. The Gulf trade that linked the Indus to Mesopotamia rose after that and unwound in its turn. The eastern-Mediterranean palace economies knit themselves tightly together across the second millennium and then came apart around 1177 BCE in the Late Bronze Age Collapse, when mainland Greece may have lost something between forty and sixty per cent of its people. The pattern was a sequence of integration-then-fragmentation cycles, the module’s rhythm in miniature, and the lesson it bequeathed to every later chapter was that integration bred fragility: the routes that carried trade also carried shocks, and a more connected system was a more exposed one.61

61 Sources: Scott (Page 1199); Kriwaczek (Page 72); Cline (Loc 507). Read more: Cline, 1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed (2014).

The second question was about channels, and this period’s answer is that all three of the module’s corridors were born here at once. The maritime Indian Ocean, the desert and oasis route that would later be called the Silk Road, and the great Eurasian steppe all took shape in these millennia.

Of the three, the steppe was the era’s dynamic engine, not its periphery: a band of unbroken grass running some seven thousand kilometres from eastern Europe to the Great Wall of China, across which mounted herders moved at a pace no one on foot could match. The ocean, the corridor this book centres on, was still largely waiting, its monsoon geography laid down but its traffic thin.

To see how the channels actually worked, it helps to follow one good through the system, and the most revealing is tin. Bronze, the period’s defining metal, needed tin, yet tin lay almost nowhere near the cities that used it, so it had to travel long distances to reach them.

Its price marked the route. Quoted in silver, 100 shekels of tin cost about 6.7 shekels of silver at Assur, about 10 at Mari and about 14.3 in Anatolia. Tin was worth roughly twice as much at the western end, and that gap pulled the metal across.

This was price-responsive long-distance trade operating centuries before any coin existed to express it, visible only in the barter ratios that the cuneiform records preserve. The volumes were not trivial either: a single cargo such as the one lost off Uluburun around 1300 BCE carried perhaps a tonne of tin in one hull.62

62 Sources: Cunliffe (Loc 158, 909); Frahm (Page 47); Anthony (Loc 6829); Cline (Loc 1646). Read more: Cunliffe, By Steppe, Desert, and Ocean (2015).

ImportantFollow the money

With no coins yet, the period’s bullion tracer was the relative price of metals, and tin is the clearest one to track. It had to travel because bronze needed it and it lay far from the cities that consumed it. The gap between its price at Assur, about 6.7 shekels of silver per 100 shekels of tin, and in Anatolia, about 14.3, moved the metal from where it was cheap toward where it was dear, centuries before coinage. The gap is also a measurement: roughly a doubling over 950 kilometres is what carriage cost in this technology, and later chapters watch that wedge narrow. This is the same “follow the metal” logic the module will use for millennia to locate the centre, except that here it shows up in barter ratios scratched onto clay rather than in the output of any mint.

It was not only raw metal that moved. High-value processed goods travelled too, and they mattered out of proportion to their bulk, because a supply that could not easily be expanded met a demand that would pay almost anything.

Ugarit, for one, paid its yearly tribute to the Hittites not only in gold but in dyed wool and finished garments, cloth being among the most concentrated stores of value a Bronze Age economy could ship. The vivid later case of such a luxury is Tyrian purple, the dye won at enormous labour from murex sea-snails, whose extreme cost made it a marker of rank for the rest of antiquity. Here a caution is needed. One scholar, Morris Silver, has read the Aegean Bronze Age textile and purple trade as evidence of a far more commercialised, market-like world than the orthodox picture allows, even casting the Trojan War as a trade war; that bolder reading is a contested hypothesis rather than settled fact, and is best treated as one provocative interpretation of the same material that more cautious accounts read as palace-administered exchange.63

63 Sources: Cline (Loc 2308); on the market-formalist reading of Aegean Bronze Age purple and textiles as a contested hypothesis, Silver, The Purpled World (2023). Read more: Cline, 1177 B.C. (2014).

The third question was about modes: what crossed the connection? Two kinds of thing did, and the chapter is clearer if they are kept apart.

On one side are real flows, and the production function names them. Output itself moves as goods. Useful knowledge moves as technique. People move as labour. Produced assets move as capital. On the other side run monetary and financial flows: things that settle a transaction, and claims that carry purchasing power across time. Silver sits on both sides at once, which is the complication this period is best at teaching.

Take them in turn, because the evidence for each is a different instrument.

Goods (Y) are somebody else’s land, arriving in usable form. A cargo of tin is a mineral endowment that Mesopotamia did not have. That is what makes tin and not grain the story: a small flow of an input nothing can substitute for has more structural consequence than a large flow of something anybody can grow.

There is also one piece of evidence here for the other side of the equation, and the archaeology of trade almost never reaches it. Demand. Ancient proteins recovered from dental calculus at Bronze Age Megiddo, around 1650 to 1320 BCE, record what individual people actually put in their mouths: cereals, sesame and dates as expected, and then soybean in one individual, turmeric in one, probable banana in one. At least one person in the second-millennium Levant ate a South Asian spice and a South Asian pulse.

Be exact about what that licenses. It establishes contact. It is silent on commerce. It cannot say how many, how often, or whether a market existed, and every exotic in the study rests on a single person. The study’s authors read it less cautiously than this and write of a pattern of established trade; the gap between what evidence licenses and what is claimed from it, including by the people who produced it, is worth more to an economist than the finding.64

64 Sources: Scott et al. (2021) on the Megiddo and Tel Erani dental calculus; the identifications rest on two and three peptides respectively, at or barely above the standard authentication threshold. Note: there is no published rebuttal of these identifications — the caution here is this book’s reading of a general methodological standard, not a critique in the literature. Read more: Scott et al., “Exotic foods reveal contact between South Asia and the Near East”, PNAS 118 (2021).

Technology (A) moved, and it moved slowly. The chariot took something like eight hundred years to cross a continent, from Sintashta graves east of the Urals before 2000 BCE to China around 1200. That is the diffusion speed of this world, and it is the baseline against which every later chapter’s should be read.

People (L) moved, and two studies measure it in a way no document can. The Harappan cemeteries are the first. Strontium and lead isotopes in tooth enamel, seventy-seven teeth from fifty-five individuals at Harappa and Farmana, and the design is what makes the result strong: the three molars form at different ages, so one skeleton yields an ordered sequence of childhood residences rather than an average. First molars fall outside the local range. Second and third converge on it, at Farmana around three to five years of age. These were not locals. They were first-generation immigrants.

The economics is in where they came from. The migrants point north-west to the upper Indus and north-east to the Punjab plains, and the lead in the Farmana burials points at the Khetri copper belt about a hundred and fifty kilometres south. Migrants and metals travelled the same corridors. Resource peripheries exported the material and the people who knew how to get it, and that is a relationship this chapter meets again at its far end, in Cornwall.

The sample will not carry a migration rate, and the reason is a selection rule rather than a small sample size. Fewer than six hundred Indus skeletons exist from a century of excavation across some 680,000 square kilometres, and formal cemetery burial is rare within that. A cemetery here is not a thin random sample of Indus society. It is a complete sample of a small institution.65

Read more: Valentine et al., PLOS ONE 10 (2015).

The second study is in Britain, and it measures a flow of people with no price attached to it at all. Five isotope systems run on 131 pigs from four Late Neolithic henge complexes in southern Britain, around 2800 to 2400 BCE. The strontium range at Durrington Walls is wider than at any other published British site, wider than a Roman legionary fortress sitting on far more varied geology. Three kilometres away, Middle Neolithic animals from West Amesbury Farm show the narrowest range of any British assemblage.

So the wide catchment is not a property of the landscape. It is new, and it is institutional.

The economics turns on the animal. Pigs drive badly over distance, and there is no reason to walk one for hundreds of kilometres rather than carry the meat, unless the animal and the people who raised it are meant to arrive together. The isotopes are therefore measuring the geographic reach of a mobilisation organised without money, writing, prices or a market. Hold it in mind for Q4: peripheral in a network is not the same thing as simple.66

Capital (K) was present and thin. Ships, pack animals, irrigation works, workshops and stores are the capital of this world, and there is nothing in it that could be called a capital market.

That last point needs care, because finance in this period was sophisticated without being broad, and the two are easy to confuse. Money and credit are not capital. They are what let capital be assembled and what bridges the delay between committing resources and getting them back.

That delay is the thing to picture. Assur to Kanesh was about 950 kilometres and roughly fifty days each way, with perhaps a hundred days between committing goods and seeing any silver return. Fifty-odd donkeys, fodder, drivers and tolls all had to be paid for in advance. A trade with that shape cannot run on cash in hand.

So it ran on credit, and we know it did because the Kanesh archive is a merchant archive: some twelve thousand private letters and account tablets from the trading families themselves rather than from a palace. For once in this period we are reading the traders. The claims and obligations in those letters are denominated in silver, across households and across a political frontier, with no coin anywhere in the system.

The institutional depth goes back further. Interest-bearing debt is securely attested from the later third millennium and may reach to about 3000 BCE; Hammurabi’s ceilings of twenty per cent on silver and thirty-three and a third on barley codified a practice that was already old. And rulers periodically wiped the slate. The earliest recorded is Enmetena of Lagash, around 2400 BCE, and the Sumerian word for what he granted was amargi, literally a return to the mother, debtors going home. It is the earliest attested word for freedom in any written language.

The first freedom in the written record is freedom from debt.

One last flow belongs here, and it complicates this book’s own spine before the spine has started. Before coinage, silver in the Levant circulated as hacksilver, cut and weighed, and because it was not endlessly remelted its lead-isotope signature reads onto the ore field that was worked. Seventy-five items from three dated Phoenician hoards show the source moving: Anatolian silver is prominent in the tenth and ninth centuries, but by the ninth-century hoard at Ein Hofez only two of twenty-nine items are consistent with Anatolian ore and most point to Iberia.

The metal is running west to east. Students arrive expecting silver always to flow toward Asia, because that is what Chapter 5 and Chapter 6 will show them. The first traceable flow in the record does the opposite. Direction is a function of where the ore sits and where the demand sits, and both of those move.67

The fourth question was about Europe, and this is the answer that has changed most.

The old line was that Bronze Age Europe sat outside the world system whose cores were in West Asia and the Indian Ocean. That is not right, and the thing that changed it is tin.

Start with why the question was stuck for so long. The obstacle was never the artefacts. It was that nobody had characterised the ores, so there was nothing to compare an ingot against. A Durham-led programme built the reference base: 142 ores analysed for trace elements, 98 for lead isotopes, 23 for tin isotopes, against more than two hundred tin artefacts. Baselines before comparisons.

Then a two-step discrimination. Cornish and Devon cassiterites carry up to 176 parts per million of indium where Iberian and French ores are under ten, and indium survives smelting, which is what makes it readable on an ingot rather than on a rock. That removes Iberia and France. Lead-isotope model age separates the two survivors: the Erzgebirge comes out at 318 to 323 million years, Cornwall and Devon at 274 to 293. Tin from three radiocarbon-dated Israeli wrecks gives 290. Rochelongue, off southern France and much later, gives 278. Both sit inside Cornwall and well outside the Erzgebirge.

One sentence keeps that honest in a seminar: tin isotopes alone cannot do this job, because the tin provinces overlap too much. The work is done by indium and by lead-isotope model age. A summary crediting “tin isotope analysis” with settling the question has misread the paper, and a good many of them do.

What is settled is the Cornish case for the Israeli wrecks and for Rochelongue. What is not settled is Uluburun, where two laboratories read the same cargo and reach incompatible answers: one programme puts the ingots inside Cornish and Devon ranges, another attributes roughly two-thirds to the Taurus and a third to Central Asia, three thousand kilometres east. Concede the strongest point against the Cornish reading rather than smoothing it — the Uluburun ingots measure 2 to 7 parts per million of indium against up to 176 in Cornish ore, and indium is the discriminator. The verdict here rests on the Israeli wrecks, not on Uluburun.68

68 Sources: Williams et al. (2025) for the Durham ore programme, the indium discrimination and the model ages; Powell et al. (2022) for the Taurus and Musiston attribution of 105 Uluburun ingots; Berger et al. (2023) for the indium measurements that cut against the Cornish reading of that cargo. Worth knowing: Berger is a co-author on two of the three, so the lines of evidence are less independent than they look, and lead-isotope provenancing at Uluburun is compromised by lead contamination running from 22 ppm to 11.5 per cent. Read more: Williams et al. (2025).

So the answer to Q4 is not “peripheral” in the sense students expect. Britain supplied tin, without which there is no bronze, to a network whose demand, capital, weights, writing and organisation all sat in West Asia. That is a resource periphery inside the system, which is a different thing from being outside it.

Make the production model concrete, because it is what makes “periphery” precise rather than dismissive. The tin came from decentralised small alluvial workings, worked part-time alongside farming, handed to coastal trading places and passed down the line. There was no direct contact of any kind between Britain and the Levant. Nobody in Cornwall knew where the metal ended up and nobody in Ugarit knew where it started. Britain supplied an input. It did not organise, price or capture the trade in it.

Which is this chapter’s sharpest instance of N as power, and the sentence is worth memorising because it recurs at Potosi, at Banda and at every commodity exporter in the twentieth century: the holder of the scarce resource was not the holder of the rents.

Now the scale, and be scrupulous with it. Perhaps 100 to 200 tonnes of tin a year across all of Europe and the Mediterranean in the mid-second millennium BCE — and say the caveat in the same breath, because the authors call the figure speculative. It is scaled from copper output at three mines on an assumed ten per cent tin-to-copper ratio, then extrapolated across more than a hundred known mines. A number built by multiplying an uncertain quantity by an assumed coefficient inherits the uncertainty of both. For proportion: Cornwall and Devon produced about 400 tonnes a year in 1200 CE on substantially the same technology with some six thousand mainly part-time workers.

A strategic flow, then, and not a large one. Both halves are true.

None of which makes Europe simple. Varna on the Black Sea, around 4500 BCE, yielded some three thousand gold objects weighing roughly six kilograms, more gold than every other fifth-millennium find in the world put together. And the Durrington pigs show southern Britain mobilising animals from most of an island to one place. Organisational sophistication and peripheral position are independent facts, and this period has both.69

Behind all four questions stood the forces this module tracks. Geography came first: the Himalayan uplift, the monsoon and the great rivers set the board. Climate was the first dynamic driver, its swings bearing on the rise and fall of cores. And technology — the sail married to monsoon knowledge, the horse, the wheel, bronze — turned geographic possibility into movement.

Which input was binding?

Every chapter in this book closes its analysis with the same line of the ledger: which input was doing the work, and which one was in the way?

N was binding, everywhere, and it bound in two different ways. As a level: there was no tin in Mesopotamia, and Uruk hauled its stone eighty kilometres, so a bronze-using city had to be connected to a continent whether it wanted to be or not. As variance: the river under the Indus cities was not short of water so much as unreliable about it. Two kinds of scarcity, one input, and the second is the one students miss.

A moved, and moved slowest. Eight hundred years for the chariot to cross a continent.

L was the residual claimant. A better technique or a windfall of land was captured as more people rather than as higher income per head. That is Malthus written in this book’s notation, and it is why the period’s thriving is counted in cities and populations.

K was real but thin, and finance was sophisticated without being broad. Credit crossed a political frontier at Assur and Kanesh centuries before anyone struck a coin. It was also relationship-based, running between known households and named agents, and there is nothing in this world that resembles an impersonal capital market.

And control of N is where the economic power sat: Dilmun taking a cut at a chokepoint without producing anything, Cyprus with its copper, Cornwall with its tin. The finding to carry forward is the one Q4 just delivered. Britain had the tin. West Asia had the trade.

The verdict: where was the centre?

The verdict for this period is that there was no single centre, and it is delivered with low confidence by design.

What the evidence shows is several loosely linked riverine cores rather than one integrated world economy with a weight that can be located. The Near East, and Uruk above all, was the most central node, a large urban demand pole and the supplier of metals into the long-distance trade. The Indus led the Gulf trade through its mature phase, and it survives as a bright spot in a thin record rather than as a documented centre.

Be exact about which way that thinness cuts. Because the humid tropics destroy organic evidence, what survives from the Indus is a lower bound on its activity rather than an estimate of it. That licenses a claim about direction — there was more there than we can see — and it licenses nothing about magnitude. What it does not license is treating the sparseness of the record as though it were evidence for the argument, which is a move this chapter made in an earlier draft and should not make. Egypt and the forming East Asian core sat largely apart from the western-Eurasian system that the evidence actually documents.70

This is where the chapter must say plainly what it cannot do. The centre-of-gravity question that runs through the rest of the book — whether the weight of the world economy sat in Asia, and whether it was moving — is not yet applicable here. This period neither supports nor contradicts the claim that Asia was central, because it predates a connected system in which a centre of gravity is even a meaningful idea. There were cores, but they were not yet bound tightly enough into one economy for the question to be asked, let alone tested. The first chapter at which the question becomes answerable is the next one, when Alexander and the Achaemenid inheritance fuse the channels into something like a single trans-Eurasian system. What this chapter contributes is therefore structural rather than evidential. It lays down the monsoon and the channel geography that will later make the Indian Ocean the natural integrator and centre the very system the book goes on to track.71

The denominator has to be kept honest. These were overwhelmingly agrarian subsistence economies in which the great majority farmed and never handled a trade good; the long-distance traffic described here was a thin and lucrative network laid over that mass, not the bulk of output. And a second caution governs what “success” can mean in this world. Whatever thriving the period shows was measured in numbers, not income per head: more food meant more people rather than richer people, the Malthusian condition humanity shared with every other species. The switch from measuring an economy by its scale to measuring it by the living standards of the people in it lies far ahead, at the Great Divergence of Chapter 7. Hold the two apart from the start: aggregate weight now, per-capita prosperity much later.72

WarningThe debate: how much weight should geography carry?

Cunliffe, and Diamond before him, lean hard on geography as the deciding force — the mountains and monsoon and grass corridors setting what was possible. The counter stresses human agency and contingency, the choices that geography permitted but did not dictate. The climate-collapse story is contested in the same way. The 4.2 ka weakening of the monsoon is invoked behind the Harappan decline, and drought behind the Late Bronze Age Collapse, yet the palaeo-record does not cleanly show a sharp, sustained drought at the right moment, so mono-causal climate accounts overreach. The sensible posture is to hold both at once: deep structures shaped the board on which the period was played, but people still chose the moves. Name the positions, weigh them, and leave the question genuinely open.

Data exhibit — the Uluburun cargo, a dataset of one. With almost no quantitative records, a single well-excavated shipwreck does the work that a price series does in later chapters. The cargo is itemised above, and Figure 1.11 plots the two metals. What you could do with this: infer how many separate economies one hull implicates, and ask what the copper-to-tin ratio implies about where the metals were sourced and where the bronze was to be made — reasoning from one rich object to a whole system, with the uncertainty stated.

Threads forward

What this chapter hands on is not a verdict but a period fully described: a world of loosely linked riverine cores, three cycles of integration and collapse, and the first evidence that connection carries shocks as well as goods. That last lesson — integration breeds fragility — runs from the Late Bronze Age Collapse through every later disintegration the book meets.73

  • Question — can a structural gravity model, fit to ancient merchant records, recover trade costs and even the geographic position of unknown trading posts?
