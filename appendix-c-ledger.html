Appendix C — The centre of gravity, chapter by chapter

Preliminary draft — under review. Published for review; content, figures and citations may still change.

This appendix collects, in one place, the running verdict each chapter entered into evidence: where the centre of economic gravity sat in that period, how confident the reading is on direction and on scale separately, and the evidence that carries it. Read down the table and the spine of the book appears as one movement — Asia central for most of the arc, a European interruption of roughly two centuries, and a partial return. The Conclusion walks these verdicts and weighs how strong the “European dominance as anomaly” reading can be made.

The table

Each row is the evidence the chapter argued for. “Where the centre sat” summarises the verdict. Direction and scale are graded separately, because they are separate claims: you can be sure which way the weight lay and quite unsure how much of it there was, and saying both is the point of the exercise. “Strongest evidence” names what carries the reading, and how much there is to carry it.

The four levels mean this:

  • high — several instruments, different biases, one answer
  • moderate — supported, but one instrument carries it
  • low — a reasonable reading; another would not be unreasonable
  • not applicable — the question has no meaning here yet

One cell carries a fifth entry. Chapter 7’s scale is marked deferred, because the series that would settle it is being rebuilt; the reason is set out below.

The centre of gravity, chapter by chapter.
Ch Period Where the centre sat Direction Scale Strongest evidence
1 c.3500–500 BCE No single centre — a multipolar world of riverine cores and steppe corridors, none of them central to the others. not applicable not applicable No one dominant productive core; trade and tribute ran between several states, none central. This is not a low grade with a missing measurement behind it: there was no connected system whose weight could be located.
2 200 BCE–500 CE Asia the production core of a multipolar relay; Rome the consumer, India the fulcrum, China the maker. Measured by demand rather than production, the peak was Rome. high low Three independent traces pointed east: Pliny’s complaint of a drain, the South-Indian coin hoards that made it physical, the Muziris papyrus that priced one cargo of it. The bullion indicated the direction without measuring it, and the magnitudes are contested — Pliny’s figures were rhetorical, and the Hermapollon cargo has been reconstructed several ways.
3 500–1000 Asia and the Islamic world central, Europe peripheral; the Abbasid–Tang high tide, with a distinctive northward silver pull to the slave-and-fur periphery. high low Four instruments agreed: city size, where money and manufactures sat, the direction of the bullion, and the direction of the trade in slaves and furs. All four fixed a region and none fixed a quantity. Baghdad at perhaps half a million was a reported estimate, and some accounts ran higher.
4 1000–1400 Asia central and measurably ahead; the Mongol overland climax re-weighted the routes without moving the centre. The first northwest-European seed showed in post-plague wages. high low Song iron output, paper money, the depth of the coin economy and cities approaching a million: four instruments with different biases, one answer. How far ahead is unmeasured — there is no output figure for the period, and the size of the Little Divergence seed was itself disputed.
5 1400–1600 Asia the silver sink; the oceans joined into one market, and New World silver ran east to buy Asian goods. moderate low The silver arbitrage: metal cheap in the Americas and Europe, dear in China, drawn east to the workshop. One instrument carries the verdict, and the module’s own rule is that bullion indicates the centre without defining it. The reading would reach high if Chinese fiscal monetisation and the price differential were argued as separate instruments; the chapter treats them as one story. The headline share — something like half of all New World silver, 1550–1645 — is a broad estimate.
6 1600–1760 Asia still the workshop on the production measure, as late as about 1750; Europe now held the armed and organised levers. On the welfare measure the two ends had already parted by about 1700. moderate low India around a quarter of world manufacturing c.1750, on the Bairoch-type estimates: one much-criticised series, with the continuing eastward silver flow as corroboration rather than as a second measure. The grade attaches to the production measure and not to the period; the welfare ratios of c.1700 returned a different answer.
7 1750–1850 The great turning point: the centre moved west, and growth switched from extensive to intensive. When it crossed is not settled — the chapter’s text says 1850, its own shares figure puts the crossing in the middle 1860s. high deferred The Great Divergence in incomes per head. That the move happened is uncontested and the direction grade reflects that. Its date and its placement both rest on the module’s centre-of-gravity locator series, which is being rebuilt, so no magnitude and no crossing date are entered here. The row is provisional.
8 1850–1914 Peak Western dominance — resting on the Indian-Ocean periphery it governed. high moderate Europe and its offshoots about 89.8 per cent of world manufactures in 1913 and Western Europe about 60.1 per cent of world exports, against India’s 1.4 per cent. The trade shares were counted; the manufacturing shares came from the same Bairoch-type family as Chapter 6, and the drain figure — above 40 per cent of Britain’s payments deficits, 1870–1915 — is a reconstruction rather than a measurement.
9 1914–1945 The order cracked, and the decisive shift ran within the West. Financial leadership passed London \(\to\) New York; the centre did not move to Asia. high moderate Western Europe’s export share fell from 60.1 per cent (1913) to 41.1 per cent (1950) as American creditor power rose, and Asia in aggregate stayed subordinate. The shares were counted; the transfer of financial leadership is read from the record rather than measured, and the interior figures run wide — twelve to eighteen million influenza deaths in India.
10 1945–2010s The return to Asia in aggregate weight: partial, recent, and inside a US-anchored order. Not a return in income per head. high high Quah’s computed centre of gravity drifts from the mid-Atlantic toward India and China; Asia’s share of world GDP rose from about 21 to 39 per cent in a single generation; China became the world’s largest manufacturer. The one period whose magnitude is computed rather than inferred — and computed on aggregate output, which is heavy in part because the populations are large.

Reading the table

Four things the table shows, and two of them are gaps.

First, the plateau and the dip. For six chapters the reading stays in the east; for three it swings hard to the west; in the last it begins to return. The European interruption was real and large, but it was an interruption in a longer pattern rather than the pattern itself.

Second, the book knows direction far better than it knows scale. Read the two grades as columns and the difference is plain. Direction is high in seven of the nine periods that return a verdict at all; scale is low in five of them and reaches high only in the final chapter. That is what the surviving evidence allows. Bullion movements, city sizes, the direction of a trade in slaves or cottons, the way a price gradient pulled: each of these tells you which way the weight lay, because each is directional by nature. None of them tells you how much. The pre-modern chapters therefore make a strong claim about where and a weak one about how large, and keeping the two apart is what this table is for.

Confidence does not rise across the book. It opens at not applicable, runs high on direction for three chapters, falls to moderate for the two silver chapters, goes unresolved at the turning point, and returns to high for the last three. What does improve, and only at the very end, is the scale column: Chapter 10 is the single row resting on a computed coordinate rather than an inference.

Third, two moves that were not a simple east–west pendulum. The centre’s pull in Chapter 3 ran north, from the Abbasid core to its slave-and-fur periphery, not east; and the decisive shift of Chapter 9 ran within the West, from London to New York, not toward Asia. A centre of gravity can move inside a region as well as between regions, and the table only reads as a clean arc if those two moves are kept in view.

Fourth, where the table is weak or silent, and why.

  • Chapters 5 and 6 each rest on one instrument. The silver-sink verdict of Chapter 5 is carried by the bullion flow alone, and the module’s own rule is that bullion indicates the centre without defining it. Chapter 6’s production verdict is carried by the Bairoch-type manufacturing estimates, and it holds for the production measure only: on welfare ratios the two ends of Eurasia had already parted by about 1700. Both chapters claim high confidence in their own text. On the scale used here, one instrument carrying a reading is moderate, and moderate is what the table records.
  • Chapter 7 is unresolved, and is left so deliberately. That the centre moved west is not in dispute. When it crossed is. The chapter’s text dates the move to 1850, its own shares figure puts the crossing in the middle 1860s, and the locator series that places the marker is being rebuilt. Until that work is finished no date and no magnitude can be entered without guessing, so none is. Read the figure’s placement of the turning point as provisional too.
  • The early and late rows are not marks on the same ruler. Chapters 1 to 6 scored aggregate weight; from Chapter 7 the measure that mattered became income per head. That switch was the Great Divergence itself, and the book announces it rather than hiding it, but it does mean a high grade in Chapter 4 and a high grade in Chapter 10 were awarded on different questions.
  • Chapter 1 is silent, and the silence is the finding. Its pair of not-applicables is not a gap in the evidence but a statement about the period: there was no connected system whose weight could be located, so the question had no meaning yet. Recording that is more useful than forcing a low grade onto a question nobody could ask.

This is a summary instrument, not a measured series. The vertical placements in the figure are stylised judgements drawn from each chapter’s verdict for Chapters 1 to 9, and the placements around the turning point are under revision; only Chapter 10’s modern decades rest on a computed centre of gravity (Quah). Treat the table as the book’s argument made auditable — every entry traceable to a chapter, and every grade traceable to the instruments behind it — rather than as a quantitative dataset.
